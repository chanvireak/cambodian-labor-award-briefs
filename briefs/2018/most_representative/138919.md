# Award No. 033/18 — Zhong Yin (Cambodia) B Textile Co., Ltd. (Arbitration Council, 27 November 2018)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators (អ៊ីង សុទ្ធី, ស៊ីន គីម ស៊ាន, គង់ ផល្ឆៈ); dissenting opinion by អ៊ីង សុទ្ធី · Doc ID 138919

## 1. Facts
Zhong Yin (Cambodia) B Textile Co., Ltd., a garment factory employing about 1,200 workers, ended the contracts of 32 to 33 workers between late 2015 and the end of 2016, most of whom had been told to resign from the Democratic Union of Apparel Workers of Cambodia (DCCWU) if they wanted their contracts renewed. [Para 298][Para 317][Para 538] The dispute covered 11 points; point 10 was settled at the hearing, the claim for Mr M under point 11 was withdrawn, and the panel considered the remaining 10. [Para 25][Para 26][Para 27] The award is binding on the parties. [Para 28][Para 879]

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. Whether the employer must reinstate the 17 fixed-term workers dismissed for refusing to leave the union, and pay their wages from termination to reinstatement? [Para 317][Para 495]
2. Whether the employer must issue piece rates for every new series in every section within 3 to 7 days? [Para 654]
3. Whether the employer must pay a 1,500 riel daily lunch allowance as in past practice? [Para 675]
4. Whether the employer must pay 10 US dollars a month for travel and accommodation as in past practice? [Para 708]
5. Whether the employer must pro-rate the 12 US dollar monthly attendance bonus for authorised personal leave? [Para 718]
6. Whether the employer must pay 5,500 riel (day shift) and 6,500 riel (night shift) meal money to the auto section? [Para 759]
7. Whether the employer must pay incentive or machine-handling money in every section as in past practice? [Para 771]
8. Whether fixed-duration contracts whose total exceeds two years must be converted into unfixed-duration contracts? [Para 783][Para 412]
9. Whether the employer must raise piece rates in line with each year's minimum wage increase? [Para 814][Para 422]
10. Whether the employer must reinstate Mr S and pay his back wages? [Para 846]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** all 17 were union members dismissed for refusing to resign from the union; many had worked over two years, so under Article 67(2) their contracts were already unfixed-duration and the endings were dismissals; the allowances were established past practice protected by Article 13 of the Labour Law. [Para 335][Para 338][Para 382][Para 415]
**Employer (respondent):** this was non-renewal of fixed-duration contracts at expiry because orders had fallen; severance of five percent was paid at each expiry; terminations covered non-union members too and the union still has over 300 members; the DCCWU is not the most-representative union and cannot bring the claim. [Para 302][Para 328][Para 332][Para 419]

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** partly granted — the employer must reinstate 17 fixed-term workers, 12 unfixed-duration workers with back wages and 2 casual workers without back wages, convert contracts exceeding two years, and apply the 16 July 2014 agreement on piece rates; the wage, allowance and bonus claims were refused. [Para 864][Para 866][Para 868][Para 876]

On jurisdiction, the panel held that Article 54 of the Law on Trade Unions reserves exclusive bargaining and dispute rights to the most-representative union, and the certificate for the Khmer Workers' Spirit Union lapsed on 13 July 2018, leaving no most-representative union in the factory. [Para 453][Para 462][Para 463] The three worker representatives nonetheless held a labour department letter of 30 August 2018, so the panel had jurisdiction to hear the case. [Para 470][Para 474] The employer's res judicata objection to points 1 to 9 failed because Award No. 032/17 had declined to consider the claim for want of standing without ruling on its merits, so the third condition for res judicata was not met. [Para 490][Para 492][Para 493]

On point 1, the panel applied Articles 12 and 279 of the Labour Law and Article 62 of the Law on Trade Unions, which forbid ending a contract, including by non-renewal, because of union membership; the employer produced no evidence of its claimed economic decline and did not dispute the workers' accounts of being asked to resign. [Para 507][Para 510][Para 517][Para 535][Para 538] It therefore found union discrimination and ordered the 17 contracts continued, but refused back wages because those contracts ended on their expiry date. [Para 545][Para 550]

On points 2 to 7 the panel applied the "past practice" doctrine in Article 65 of the Labour Law and Award No. 95/09: a practice may end where circumstances genuinely change or the parties agree. [Para 675][Para 687][Para 688] It ordered compliance with points 2 and 11 of the 16 July 2014 agreement on issuing piece rates 3 to 7 days after a new series [Para 670][Para 674], but refused the lunch, travel, meal and incentive claims, finding the fall in orders and rising minimum wage had changed the original circumstances and that the employer had consulted the union before ending them. [Para 703][Para 704][Para 779]

On point 5 the panel followed the Full Bench decision of 3 October 2017 on Notice No. 230: only statutory, contractual and legally compelled absences count as attendance, and beyond those the employer retains discretion over the bonus. [Para 721][Para 752][Para 756]

On point 8 the panel held that successive three-month contracts exceeding two years convert into unfixed-duration contracts under Article 67(2), and that the five percent severance paid at each expiry does not prevent conversion, following Award No. 91/10. [Para 785][Para 805][Para 811][Para 812] It ordered the conversion. [Para 813]

On point 9 the panel found no law, collective agreement or practice requiring piece rates to track the minimum wage; the claim was an interest dispute, which only a most-representative union may bring, so it declined to consider it. [Para 825][Para 841][Para 845]

On point 10 the panel dismissed the claim: the worker joined the union after his casual contract had already ended, and the employer was entitled to end a casual contract early when work ran out. [Para 620][Para 622][Para 623]

## 5. Ratio decidendi
Ending a fixed-duration contract at expiry because the worker refused to resign from a union is discrimination under Articles 12 and 279 of the Labour Law and Article 62 of the Law on Trade Unions, and the panel will order the contract continued even though it had run its term, but will not award wages for the gap because the employment relationship ended on the expiry date; a chain of fixed-duration contracts exceeding two years converts to unfixed duration and the five percent severance paid at each expiry does not prevent it. [Para 517][Para 545][Para 550][Para 805][Para 812]

**Obiter dicta:** the panel observed that the Labour Law leans toward unfixed-duration contracts for the job security they give, and that ILO Recommendation No. 166 of 1982, though not binding, is useful context; it also held that an interest dispute may be brought only by a most-representative union because an award on interests replaces the collective agreement for a year. [Para 793][Para 796][Para 837][Para 839]

## 6. Source defects disclosed
The most-representative union provisions are badly corrupted by OCR: paragraph 465 reads "COVID" inside the text of Prakas No. 303 and paragraphs 466 to 471 are fragments. [Para 465][Para 466] The worker count is unstable — the claim names 32 workers while the same paragraph says the employer ended the contracts of "all 33 workers above", and paragraph 601 speaks of "30 workers" before naming three. [Para 317][Para 601] The past-practice period is printed as "2013 to December 2021 2016" at paragraph 691. [Para 691] The 17-worker list is spelled inconsistently between the reasoning and the order, and the deceased worker's claim appears under two different spellings of his name. [Para 364][Para 495][Para 635] The dispositive text for point 11 is garbled at paragraph 878, and internal page numbers are embedded mid-sentence throughout. [Para 878][Para 870]
