# Award No. 182/11 — MPA Security Company (Arbitration Council, 13 February 2012)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · three-member panel (Seng Vouch Hun, employer-appointed; Tuon Si Phan, worker-appointed; Pen Bun Chhea, president) · Doc ID 140120

## 1. Facts
MPA Security Company supplies guards in Phnom Penh and employs about 2,000 staff; four former guards, represented by the Democratic Federation of Khmer Workers' Unions, brought seven demands on conditions of work [Para 56][Para 57]. Two were withdrawn at the hearing, leaving four: unpaid March 2011 final wages and the repayment of a USD 20 deduction, overtime for one guard, Sunday and public-holiday pay, and unused annual leave [Para 28][Para 29]. The four guards resigned by letter given on 25 March 2011 for a departure on 1 April 2011, six days later, and one of them then worked a further eleven days under a new contract before leaving again [Para 60][Para 68][Para 90]. The company withheld the last month's wages on the ground that the guards left without one month's notice and without a reason, as its internal rules require [Para 62][Para 91].

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. Whether the company must pay the four guards their final March 2011 wages, and repay the USD 20 deducted from one of them as a penalty for sleeping on duty? [Para 58][Para 72]
2. Whether the company must pay overtime to the guard who claims 1,900 minutes of extra work in December 2010 and January 2011? [Para 76][Para 118]
3. Whether the company must pay overtime at the enhanced rate for work on Sundays and public holidays, which the guards say they never took off? [Para 79][Para 126]
4. Whether the company must pay compensation for annual leave that the four guards never used? [Para 85][Para 136]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** The four left on 1 April 2011 and have still not been paid their March wages; one worked eleven more days afterwards [Para 61][Para 90]. The company deducted USD 20 from one guard's February 2011 wages because he slept while on duty at his post [Para 72][Para 73]. They worked seven days a week, thirty days a month, without Sunday or public-holiday rest and without correct overtime, claiming 1,900 minutes of extra work for one guard [Para 76][Para 81]. They never received annual leave during their employment [Para 85].
**Employer (respondent):** The internal rules require one month's notice, and the guards gave six days and no reason, so the final wages were withheld [Para 62][Para 91]. The USD 20 deduction was a warning applied under the company's published disciplinary scale to any guard who sleeps on duty [Para 74][Para 75]. It admits that in recent months guards worked seven days a week without Sunday rest because the company was short of staff, but says holiday pay was given, though calculated incorrectly [Para 82][Para 84].

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** partly granted — the company was ordered to pay the four guards their March 2011 final wages calculated on the contract base wage, to repay the USD 20 deducted from the guard who slept on duty, to pay Sunday overtime and public-holiday work at 200 per cent, and to pay compensation for unused annual leave; the claim for USD 7.73 of overtime was dismissed, with the overtime to be recalculated on the correct daily base of USD 55 divided by 26 days [Para 144][Para 145][Para 146][Para 147][Para 148][Para 149][Para 150]

On issue 1 the panel asked whether the guards owed one month's notice. Articles 74 and 78 allow either party to end an indefinite contract by written notice, and Article 75 fixes the minimum at fifteen days for service between six months and two years, which matched all four [Para 92][Para 95][Para 99][Para 101][Para 102]. They gave six days, so the notice period was not respected [Para 103][Para 104]. The company relied on its internal rules, but the panel, applying the principle that the party asserting a fact bears the burden of proof, found no clause in those rules withholding final wages for short notice [Para 106][Para 108]. Article 116 then requires all wages and allowances to be paid within 48 hours of the last working day, and the wages had not been paid [Para 109][Para 110]. On the USD 20, Article 28 forbids employers to impose monetary penalties, defining them as any measure reducing the wage the worker would normally earn [Para 114][Para 115]. The deduction of February 2011 was therefore unlawful and had to be repaid [Para 116][Para 117].

On issue 2 the panel dismissed the claim as quantified, because the payroll showed the guard had already received USD 5.43 for December 2010 and USD 7.24 for January 2011 [Para 77][Para 119][Para 120]. It nonetheless found the company's whole method wrong: it divided USD 48.85 instead of the contractual base of USD 55 by 26 days, understating the daily rate [Para 121][Para 122]. Under Article 139 the daily base is to be multiplied by the overtime hours and by 1.5, and the case was disposed of on that footing rather than by an award of the sum claimed [Para 123][Para 124][Para 125].

On issue 3 the panel found that the four had never had a Sunday off and had not received correct Sunday overtime, the company having admitted the seven-day weeks [Para 129][Para 130]. Article 139 requires 200 per cent for work on the weekly rest day, and Prakas No. 10 of 4 February 1999 requires the same for public holidays [Para 128][Para 132][Para 133]. The company had calculated holiday pay incorrectly, so the panel ordered recalculation on the correct daily base for one Sunday per month over each guard's length of service [Para 134][Para 135]. On issue 4 it held that a year of service carries eighteen days of paid annual leave, and that Article 167 requires payment for leave unused when the contract ends [Para 139][Para 140][Para 143].


## 5. Ratio decidendi
An employer may not deduct wages as a disciplinary fine, whatever its internal rules provide: Article 28 makes any reduction of the wage normally earned unlawful, and the sum must be repaid. An employer that underpays overtime by dividing a sub-minimum figure rather than the contractual base wage must recalculate at the contractual base, multiplied by the hours and by the statutory premium, and the worker's own arithmetic does not fix the amount owed [Para 115][Para 117][Para 122][Para 124].

**Obiter dicta:** The panel's remark that the guards remain free to challenge the notice period under the Civil Procedure Code, and its comment that the company's daily-wage formula produced a wrong figure, were not necessary to the orders [Para 133][Para 121].

## 6. Source defects disclosed
The overtime claim is quantified three ways: 1,900 minutes and USD 7.73 in the facts, 1,190 minutes in the reasoning, and "USD 73" in the same line, and 1,900 minutes is converted into "19 hours 83 minutes", which is arithmetically impossible [Para 76][Para 118]. The months are printed as December 2010 and "January 2019" in the claim and January 2011 in the reasoning [Para 76][Para 77]. The award date is 13 February 2012 on the face, yet the panel cites a protection order of 16 December 2011 and a hearing of 8 February 2012 as if earlier [Para 1][Para 21]. The company's name appears as MPA, "MPA International" and four different Khmer transliterations [Para 40][Para 56]. The signature block names only two of the three arbitrators [Para 152]. The claim of a worker-free annual-leave schedule is recorded against the company's account of a December 2011 rota without any document resolving it [Para 86][Para 88].
