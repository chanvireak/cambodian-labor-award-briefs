# Award No. 184/12 — ក្រុមហ៊ុន ណាហ្គាវើលដ៍ លីមីធីត (Arbitration Council, 01 November 2012)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · ម៉ារ សំបូរ ណា, អន ណន, គង់ ផល្បៈ · Doc ID 140184

## 1. Facts
The dispute arose from a collective labour complaint against the employer at NagaWorld, where about 4,250 workers were employed [Para 36]. The worker side was the Khmer Employees Workers' Labour Rights Union, with CTSWF also named [Para 37]. The employer was represented by the HR manager and HR executives [Para 1]. Two matters remained unresolved after conciliation: reinstatement and back pay for three union members, and the number of employee representative seats [Para 2] [Para 4]. At the hearing, the second issue was settled, leaving only the first for decision [Para 17] [Para 18]. The panel considered documents from both sides and the Ministry, but no witnesses or experts were called [Para 20]. The three workers had long service: Ms A and Ms C nearly twelve years, and Mr B about two years and seven months [Para 51] [Para 68] [Para 79]. The panel treated all three as indefinite-term contracts [Para 51] [Para 68] [Para 79].

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. (Issue 1) Whether the employer was required to take back the three named union members and pay back wages, or instead pay termination benefits after their dismissal? [Para 2] [Para 40]
2. (Issue 1) Whether the employer had reasonable cause to dismiss Ms A for repeated medium-level misconduct under the internal rules? [Para 50] [Para 100]
3. (Issue 1) Whether the employer had reasonable cause to dismiss Mr B for serious misconduct involving missing chips, despite dismissing him sixteen days after the incident? [Para 69] [Para 134] [Para 137]
4. (Issue 1) Whether the employer had reasonable cause to dismiss Ms C for allegedly soliciting personal tips from a customer? [Para 80] [Para 89]
5. (Issue 1) What severance, notice, leave, final-wage and moral-damages sums were payable to each worker? [Para 102] [Para 140] [Para 168]

## 3. Positions of the parties
The union initially sought reinstatement of the three workers to their former positions and back pay [Para 2]. At the hearing, it changed the claim to payment of termination benefits for all three [Para 40]. It claimed monthly average wages of USD 476 for Ms A, USD 745 for Mr B and USD 461.84 for Ms C, relying on bank books [Para 41] [Para 42] [Para 43] [Para 44]. The panel did not consider those bank books because they were in a foreign language [Para 44]. The employer refused reinstatement, saying the three had breached internal rules [Para 3]. It relied on its internal regulations dated 08 August 2010 [Para 23] [Para 64]. F. M. A, it alleged repeated medium misconduct, including warnings, suspensions and a final incident on 10 July 2012 [Para 52] [Para 57] [Para 100]. For Mr B, it alleged negligence causing five chips worth USD 5,000 to go missing, plus a similar prior warning [Para 69] [Para 71]. F. M. C, it alleged customer complaint and suspected personal solicitation of tips [Para 81] [Para 89]. The employer produced starting wages of USD 80 monthly but did not show current wages [Para 45] [Para 47].

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** partly granted — the employer was ordered to pay termination-related amounts in kind and time periods, not USD sums: Ms A received six months' wages and benefits as severance, three months' wages and benefits as notice pay, remaining annual leave for the last three years, final wages within 48 hours, and no moral damages [Para 111] [Para 115] [Para 123] [Para 125] [Para 131]. Mr B received 45 days' wages and benefits as severance, one month's wages and benefits as notice pay, remaining annual leave, final wages within 48 hours, and no moral damages [Para 144] [Para 149] [Para 150] [Para 151] [Para 158]. Ms C received six months' wages and benefits as severance, three months' wages and benefits as notice pay, remaining annual leave for the last three years, final wages within 48 hours, and moral damages equal to six months' wages and benefits [Para 172] [Para 177] [Para 179] [Para 180] [Para 183]. The panel held that indefinite-term contracts may be terminated unilaterally but require written notice and reasonable cause related to conduct or enterprise needs [Para 96] [Para 97] [Para 98]. Ms A' repeated medium misconduct gave reasonable cause, but because it was not serious misconduct, severance remained due [Para 101] [Para 107] [Para 108]. Mr B' negligence was serious misconduct, but the employer lost the right to discipline by acting after the seven-day period [Para 135] [Para 136] [Para 138]. Ms C' dismissal was unjustified because the employer only suspected tip solicitation and produced no specific proof [Para 161] [Para 165] [Para 166].

## 5. Ratio decidendi
A future panel should ask first whether the employer proved the alleged misconduct with specific evidence; suspicion alone cannot justify dismissal [Para 161] [Para 165]. Even proved serious misconduct must be sanctioned within the statutory seven-day period, or the right to dismiss on that ground is lost [Para 136] [Para 138]. Where dismissal is lawful but based on medium rather than serious misconduct, statutory severance remains payable [Para 101] [Para 107] [Para 108]. Article 89 governs severance for indefinite-term contracts terminated by the employer [Para 89] [Para 102]. Article 91 moral damages require termination without proper reason [Para 91] [Para 126] [Para 130]. **Obiter dicta:** The panel referred to earlier award No. 113/11-Yongwa II for the proposition that the claimant bears the burden of sufficient proof [Para 164].
