# Award No. 13/12 — Va Yang Garment (Cambodia) Co., Ltd. (Arbitration Council, 24 February 2012)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators — Ing Sothy (employer-appointed), Liv Sovanna (worker-appointed), Sokh Mathoeung (president) · Doc ID 139940

## 1. Facts
Va Yang Garment (Cambodia) Co., Ltd., operating since 2007 at Trapeang Chhouk, Tek Thla, Sen Sok, Phnom Penh, employed about 450 workers, of whom the Khmer Workers' Consciousness Union held 142 members and registration from the Ministry of Labour dated 16 January 2012 [Para 35][Para 36][Para 37]. Of eight claims filed in December 2011, seven were settled in conciliation and one remained: the workers' demand that the company remove Mr D (name redacted), the factory interpreter and assistant to the director, for serious misconduct under Art. 83 of the Labour Law, after three incidents involving Ms S (name redacted), the union president and cutting-section head [Para 2][Para 11][Para 38]. The panel heard the case on 6 and 9 February 2012 and issued a non-binding award [Para 8][Para 9][Para 17].

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. Whether the workers have the right to require the employer to dismiss the interpreter, a fellow employee, for insults and threats directed at the union president? [Para 38][Para 72]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** the interpreter insulted and threatened Ms S three times — on 22 November 2011 threatening to kick her down from the second floor, on 28 November 2011 asking what right she had to come upstairs and having her removed, and on 19 January 2012 accusing her of calling workers out at 4 pm and cursing her with "may a car hit your motorbike and may you lose a child" — conduct amounting to serious misconduct under Art. 83 [Para 41][Para 42][Para 47][Para 58].
**Employer (respondent):** it cannot dismiss him but may warn him; the November incidents were jokes at which everyone laughed, he did not know she took offence and would apologise; it had no knowledge of the January incident but had already spoken to him orally about his language [Para 39][Para 49][Para 51][Para 52][Para 53].

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** dismissed — the claim for the interpreter's dismissal was refused; no sum in riel or USD was awarded [Para 101][Para 102].

The panel began from the contract, not the misconduct. Under Art. 65 of the Labour Law a labour contract is a relationship between worker and employer, and under Arts. 664 and 311 of the 2007 Civil Code a contract is formed, modified and extinguished only by the will of its parties; a third party outside the contract cannot vary or terminate it [Para 72][Para 74][Para 77][Para 78][Para 79]. Since the interpreter held his own contract with the employer, the workers were third parties to it [Para 83][Para 84]. Following Awards No. 54/08 (Chung Yeu) and No. 124/10 (Chun Pichha), the panel held that workers cannot order or demand the dismissal or transfer of an employee unless they prove that the person is dangerous and cannot be kept in the company, so that retaining him would create a specific workplace insecurity [Para 80][Para 81][Para 85].

Turning to that exception, the panel weighed the evidence. Two worker witnesses said the interpreter challenged Ms S to swear an oath using the words about the car and the child, one from about two metres away and one from about five metres behind a wall; the employer's witness said the two shouted and pointed at each other and always quarrelled, and that the words were an invitation to swear rather than an insult [Para 57][Para 59][Para 61][Para 63][Para 66]. The panel found the language improper whatever its purpose, since it could affect feelings and workplace relations, but accepted that on 19 January 2012 it was a demand that she swear an oath [Para 93][Para 98]. It noted the employer had already warned him orally over the November incidents [Para 96]. On that record it found him not to be a person whose retention would cause specific workplace insecurity, and refused the dismissal [Para 100][Para 101].

## 5. Ratio decidendi
A worker or union that is not a party to another employee's labour contract cannot require the employer to dismiss that employee; the panel will entertain such a demand only where the claimants prove the person is dangerous and cannot be retained without creating a specific insecurity at the workplace, and even then the remedy belongs to the employer's proportionate disciplinary choice, not to the claimants [Para 80][Para 85].

**Obiter dicta:** the panel observed that improper or insulting language is itself inappropriate conduct that can cause stagnation in the workplace, and that the employer should adopt an appropriate measure to stop it and to guarantee the interpreter will not use such words again [Para 94][Para 95][Para 99]; it added that Ms S should not have used the same words back at him [Para 97].

## 6. Source defects disclosed
The hearing record prints the 19 January 2012 date as "19 January 2017 2012", giving two years for the same event [Para 89]. The signature block repeats the employer-appointed arbitrator's name twice and carries OCR fragments in place of the other signatures [Para 103]. Party names in the OCR are unstable — the union appears as ស្មារតី and ស្ទារតី, the company as វ៉ា យ៉ាង and រ៉ា យ៉ាង — and one paragraph of the second hearing is reduced to a stray Latin string [Para 2][Para 16]. The award number appears only in the header and the appendix does not survive [Para 1]. The two individual names central to the dispute are preserved in the source but are redacted here as Mr D and Ms S.
