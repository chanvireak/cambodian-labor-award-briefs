# Award No. 178/11 — Huy Chhean (Cambodia) Co., Ltd. (Arbitration Council, 30 January 2012)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · panel of three arbitrators (Ing Sothy; Sin Kim Sean; Kong Phallha) · employer-appointed member dissenting in part · Doc ID 140119

## 1. Facts
Huy Chhean (Cambodia) Co., Ltd. is a Phnom Penh shoe manufacturer with about 2,500 workers, five of whose demands were pressed by the Democratic Union of Cambodian Garment Workers [Para 67][Para 16]. On 5 and 6 December 2011 the company ordered 35 pattern-room technicians to move to an affiliated branch, Maru Chun, and removed their equipment when they refused; they sat in their old workplace until 11 December and struck from 12 to 16 December [Para 73][Para 74][Para 79]. On 16 December the Phnom Penh First Instance Court issued a protection order requiring them to return within 48 hours; the workers say they received it only on 19 December, by which time the 48 hours had run, and the company declared them to have abandoned their jobs and dismissed all 35 on 20 December for serious misconduct [Para 80][Para 86][Para 87][Para 88]. A separate demand concerned 67 planning-section workers whose daily wages and USD 7 attendance bonus were deducted for 27 October 2011, when a section head told them to sit idle [Para 100][Para 101].

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. Whether the company must keep the 35 technicians at their original workplace or, failing that, terminate their contracts and pay the statutory termination benefits? [Para 70][Para 90]
2. Whether the company must restore the wages and the USD 7 attendance bonus deducted from the 67 planning workers for 27 October 2011? [Para 92][Para 162]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** The transfer was imposed without prior notice or discussion, the new site is far away and travel is unsafe, and the workers were never told what benefits they would keep; they struck only after these failures [Para 79]. They no longer seek reinstatement but ask for termination with notice pay, dismissal compensation, moral damages, final wages and unused annual leave [Para 90][Para 91]. On the deduction, the section head himself told them to sit and do nothing, and the company promised at a meeting with two buyer representatives that their wages and bonus would be preserved [Para 96][Para 104].
**Employer (respondent):** The move answered the buyer's requirement that technical, planning and sewing sections sit in one production chain, which the rented six-storey building could not accommodate [Para 76][Para 77]. The dismissal rests on serious misconduct: the workers disobeyed the court's protection order, which took effect immediately [Para 88][Para 91]. It denies promising to preserve the wages and bonus [Para 106].

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** partly granted — the dismissal of the 35 technicians was upheld as based on serious misconduct, with no notice pay, no dismissal compensation and no moral damages, and the company was ordered to pay only their unpaid final wages for 1–5 December 2011 and unused annual leave; the claim of the 67 planning workers to retain their wages and USD 7 attendance bonus for 27 October 2011 was dismissed and the deduction confirmed pro rata [Para 161][Para 176][Para 177][Para 178]

The panel classified both demands as rights disputes because they turn on provisions of the Labour Law [Para 116]. On issue 1 it first had to decide the effect of the protection order. Article 337 empowers only the labour court, or the ordinary court where none exists, to rule on the legality of a strike, and provides that workers who do not return within 48 hours of a lawful declaration commit serious misconduct [Para 121][Para 123]. Article 562(3) of the Civil Procedure Code, and the commentary on it, make service of the protection order unnecessary for its enforcement where urgency and confidentiality require it [Para 128][Para 129]. The panel therefore held that late receipt did not release the workers from the duty to obey, since paragraph 3 of the order stated that it took effect immediately [Para 130][Para 131]. It added that some workers knew of the order before 19 December [Para 127]. Their failure to return was serious misconduct, and the parallel challenge to the protection order was outside the panel's jurisdiction as it was not a labour dispute [Para 132][Para 134][Para 135]. Because Article 82 excuses notice in cases of serious misconduct, no notice pay was due [Para 154][Para 156]; Article 89 likewise excludes dismissal compensation where the worker is at fault, and Article 91 requires a wrongful termination before moral damages can be claimed, so neither was awarded [Para 157][Para 159][Para 160][Para 161]. The workers were nevertheless entitled to wages for the days actually worked in December and to payment for unused annual leave under Articles 116 and 167 [Para 143][Para 150][Para 151][Para 153].

On issue 2 the panel held that the USD 7 attendance bonus is part of wages under Article 103 and Notification No. 041, being a reward for a full month's attendance [Para 167][Para 168][Para 169]. It then reasoned that depriving the 67 workers of the bonus entirely would be unjust, but paying it for a day not worked would be equally unjust to the employer [Para 171][Para 172]. Since the contract of employment is suspended while the worker does not perform, under Articles 72 and 332 the employer owes no wage and no bonus for that day [Para 162][Para 148][Para 166][Para 173]. The deduction, made in proportion to the days not worked, was therefore lawful and the claim to restore it was dismissed [Para 174][Para 175].

## 5. Ratio decidendi
A court protection order that states it takes effect immediately binds the workers from the moment it is issued, and failure to return within the 48 hours fixed by Article 337 is serious misconduct even where the workers received the order after that period had expired; serious misconduct removes notice pay, dismissal compensation and moral damages but not wages for days worked or unused annual leave. A regular-attendance bonus is part of wages, yet because the employment contract is suspended on a day not worked the employer may deduct it pro rata for that day [Para 130][Para 159][Para 173].

**Obiter dicta:** The panel's remark that losing the whole bonus would be unjust to the workers while paying it for an unworked day would be unjust to the employer, and its observation that some workers knew of the order before 19 December, were not necessary to the outcome [Para 127][Para 171].

## 6. Source defects disclosed
The award is dated 30 January 2012 on the face and at the signature block, but one evidence entry is dated 17 October 2012, after the award, and the memorandum of understanding is printed "ឆ្នាំ ២០១០០" [Para 1][Para 45][Para 22]. The court order is described as allowing 48 hours and then glossed in words as "eighty-eight hours" [Para 80][Para 118]. The head-count of the technicians shifts between 35 and "20 35", and one line speaks of compensation for "4 workers" among the 35 [Para 145][Para 153]. The wage table listing the 35 claimants is badly fragmented, with amounts and names repeated and dates run together, so individual figures cannot be relied on [Para 70][Para 71]. The dispute number appears as 178/11 on the face, "150/11" in the union's complaint and "005/11" in a union letter [Para 1][Para 16][Para 56]. One date reads "27 October 2019 2011" [Para 170].
