# Award No. 15/12 — SD (Cambodia) Co., Ltd. (Arbitration Council, 21 February 2012)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators — Chhiv Phirem (employer-appointed), An Non (worker-appointed), Run Saray (president) · Doc ID 139942

## 1. Facts
SD (Cambodia) Co., Ltd. employs about 720 workers at Ang Snoul, Dangkor, Phnom Penh; the National Union of the Garment Industry of Cambodia, the claimant, claimed about 399 members [Para 33]. On 26 December 2011 the union filed 14 claims to improve working conditions; on 17 January 2012 four remained unresolved and were referred to the panel, which heard the case on 9 February 2012 [Para 16][Para 17][Para 18][Para 15]. At the hearing three of those four were disposed of — Issues 1 and 4 withdrawn by the workers and Issue 2 settled by agreement — leaving Issue 3 alone: the employer's refusal to let workers suspend their contracts to take long-term sick leave [Para 21].

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. (Issue 1) Whether the employer must pay a USD 5 monthly travel allowance? [Para 3]
2. (Issue 2) Whether pregnant workers must be given two days a month, with wages and benefits maintained, to attend antenatal checks? [Para 5]
3. (Issue 3) Whether the employer must allow a worker to suspend the labour contract to rest and treat a long illness certified by an official doctor? [Para 34]
4. (Issue 4) Whether the employer must pay USD 15 a month in lieu of a nursery, as the workers claimed, or USD 6 as the employer offered? [Para 9][Para 10]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** a USD 5 monthly travel allowance is owed; pregnant workers need two paid days a month for health checks; and, above all, the employer must honour Art. 71(3) and permit suspension of the contract for long illness [Para 3][Para 5][Para 34]. In practice it refuses: it grants only two or three days, or at most one week, and in September 2011 refused a worker time off for surgery and home treatment, in December 2011 refused one who had an appendectomy so that she resigned, and in January 2012 refused a team leader eye surgery [Para 35][Para 36][Para 37][Para 39][Para 40].
**Employer (respondent):** it cannot afford the USD 5 travel money; it already gives pregnant workers three days across the nine months and ten days at full wages and benefits; it does not permit suspension of the contract at all; and the decision on any such request lies with the production head, or the finishing-section manager, with the administration taking a refused letter to the company head for a final ruling [Para 4][Para 6][Para 8][Para 42][Para 43][Para 44].

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** granted (Issue 3) — the employer was ordered to allow suspension of the labour contract where illness is certified by an official doctor, for up to six months and extendable until a replacement is found; no sum in riel or USD was awarded [Para 63][Para 64]. Issues 1 and 4 had been withdrawn and Issue 2 agreed [Para 21].

The panel first classified Issue 3 as a rights dispute, since suspension of the employment contract on illness is governed by Art. 71(3) of the Labour Law and a rights dispute is one concerning entitlements under the law, an agreement or a collective agreement, following Awards 05/11, 13/11 and 14/11 [Para 45][Para 46][Para 48]. Art. 71(3) provides that the contract is suspended by the absence of a worker in case of illness duly certified by an official doctor, and that the absence may run up to six months, extendable until a replacement is found [Para 49][Para 50].

The panel found as fact that the employer did not permit workers to suspend their contracts when they asked for sick leave to obtain treatment, and that it did not contest the three concrete cases put to it — the surgery in September 2011, the appendectomy in December 2011 and the eye operation in January 2012 [Para 51][Para 41][Para 37][Para 38][Para 40]. Following Award No. 62/04 (E Sin), it held that Art. 71(3) requires the employer to allow at least six months' suspension before terminating the contract [Para 53][Para 54]. Illness was, it observed, neither foreseeable nor voluntary on the worker's part, and it is what prevents attendance at work [Para 55].

The panel then applied Art. 13: provisions of the Labour Law are public order, and any rule arising from a unilateral employer decision, a contract or a collective agreement that does not respect them is void by operation of law [Para 57][Para 58]. The employer's unilateral refusal to allow suspension for treatment was therefore not in accordance with Art. 71(3) and was void [Para 59][Para 60]. It ordered the employer to permit suspension on production of a proper medical certificate, for up to six months and extendable until a replacement [Para 61][Para 62][Para 63][Para 64]. The award on this rights dispute is final and immediately enforceable between the parties under the MoU of 28 September 2010 [Para 65].

## 5. Ratio decidendi
Suspension of the labour contract for illness is a statutory entitlement, not a matter of managerial permission: where an official doctor duly certifies the illness, the contract stands suspended for up to six months, extendable until a replacement is engaged, and a unilateral refusal to allow the suspension is void under Art. 13 because the Labour Law's provisions are public order [Para 49][Para 59][Para 60]. A claim founded on that entitlement is a rights dispute, so the resulting award binds the parties without the eight-day objection period [Para 48][Para 65].

**Obiter dicta:** the panel noted that during suspension the worker receives no wages because no work is performed for the employer under Art. 72(1) [Para 56], and it recorded the employer's internal routing of suspension requests through the production or finishing-section head to administration and the company head without endorsing it [Para 42][Para 44].

## 6. Source defects disclosed
The subject line of the source is corrupted by OCR noise before the Council's name [Para 1]. The workers' Issue 2 demand is printed as "one day two" per month — "មួយ ថ្ងៃទី ២ ក្នុង មួយ ខែ" — which cannot be reconciled with the employer's answer that it grants "three days" over nine months and ten days; the numeral is unreliable in both places [Para 5][Para 6]. A passage of the rights-dispute analysis is reduced to an English OCR fragment embedded in the Khmer text [Para 24]. The arbitrator's signature line for the president carries stray Latin characters, and the panel's names appear only in the header and the signature block [Para 67]. Figures for the workforce (about 720) and union membership (about 399) come from a single paragraph and cannot be cross-checked [Para 33].
