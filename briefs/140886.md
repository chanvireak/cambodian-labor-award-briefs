# Award No. 007/24 — Hung Sing Garment (Cambodia) Co., Ltd. (ក្រុមហ៊ុន ហ៊ុង ស៊ីង ហ្គាម៉ិន (ខេមបូឌា) ឯ.ក) (Arbitration Council, 10 June 2024)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators (អ៉ីង សុទ្ធី, លីវ សុវណ្ណា, ប៉ែន ប៊ុនឈា) · Doc ID 140886

## 1. Facts
Hung Sing Garment (Cambodia) Co., Ltd. (ក្រុមហ៊ុន ហ៊ុង ស៊ីង ហ្គាម៉ិន (ខេមបូឌា) ឯ.ក) is a garment factory in Kien Svay district, Kandal, employing 1,307 workers [Para 48]. The Khmer Workers' Spirit Union Federation (សហព័ន្ធ សហជីព ស្មារតី កម្មករ ខ្មែរ) is the only union at the factory but does not hold most-representative status [Para 54]. Of six demands raised on 23 March 2024, three were settled in conciliation, two were routed to the labour inspectorate, and one — a midday meal — was forwarded to the Arbitration Council [Para 10][Para 13][Para 14]. The complaint carried 787 thumbprints, which the panel reduced to 772 valid claimants [Para 50][Para 73].

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. (Issue 1) Whether the employer is obliged to provide one midday meal, or 4000 riel per worker in lieu, to the 772 claimant workers? [Para 75][Para 93]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** the union conceded that no law fixes a midday meal or meal allowance, but argued the benefit is needed so workers can buy adequate food, that it would raise productivity above target, and that most factories already provide it [Para 55][Para 57][Para 58]. The employer's recent hiring of six new workers and overtime work showed it had recovered, so it should pay [Para 59][Para 60].

**Employer (respondent):** it has no capacity to pay a meal allowance because its finances remain weak, overtime is uneven across sections, and benefits differ between factories according to each management's strategy [Para 61][Para 62]. Its headcount fell from over 1600 to 1311 and its sewing lines from 20 to 15, so new hires reflect sectional need, not recovery [Para 62][Para 63][Para 64]. In its written statement it maintained that wages, seniority bonuses, attendance bonuses and accommodation and travel allowances were all paid in accordance with the Labour Law [Para 65][Para 66].

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** dismissed — the panel declined to consider the claim for one midday meal or 4000 riel per worker for the 772 claimants [Para 93][Para 94]

The panel found no provision of the Labour Law, no collective agreement, no internal rule and no past practice creating a duty to supply a meal or a 4000 riel allowance [Para 75][Para 76]. It characterised the claim as a demand for an extra benefit lying outside any legal or contractual source, and therefore as an interest dispute rather than a rights dispute [Para 76][Para 77].

That characterisation drove the outcome. Under Article 96 §2 of the 1997 Labour Law a collective agreement is a written bargain between an employer or employers' organisation and a trade union representing workers [Para 78][Para 80]. Under Article 54-new of the amended Trade Union Law only a most-representative union, or one meeting the statutory thresholds, may conclude a collective agreement or settle a collective labour dispute [Para 81][Para 82]. Where no union can meet those criteria, Article 72 requires a bargaining council [Para 84].

The panel then applied Article 43 §1 of Prakas No. 099, under which an award resolving an interest dispute substitutes for a collective agreement and takes effect for one year [Para 86]. Because such an award performs the function of a collective agreement, the panel reasoned it may only be issued at the request of a union that is most representative under Article 54-new or sufficiently qualified under Article 72 [Para 88][Para 85]. The claimant was neither, so the panel declined to consider the demand at all [Para 89][Para 91][Para 92]. It followed earlier awards including No. 118/16-E-Rchang (Cambodia) and No. 203/16-International Union Enterprise [Para 90].

## 5. Ratio decidendi
An arbitration panel will not adjudicate an interest dispute — a claim for a benefit grounded in no law, collective agreement, internal rule or established practice — where the claimant is not a most-representative union under Article 54-new of the Trade Union Law and meets no alternative qualification under Article 72, because an award on such a dispute would stand in place of a collective agreement the claimant has no power to conclude [Para 88][Para 91][Para 86].

**Obiter dicta:** the panel noted that the two further unsettled demands recorded in the conciliation minutes had not been referred to it, and that it would have examined its jurisdiction over them had the workers asked [Para 21][Para 22]. It also observed that the only union at the factory is not most representative, a fact relevant to future interest disputes [Para 54].

## 6. Source defects disclosed
Headcount is printed inconsistently: 1,307 workers in the findings and 1,311 in the parties' agreed facts [Para 48][Para 53]. The claimant count shifts from 787 thumbprints to 788 in the demand paragraph and settles at 772 after the panel deducted unnamed, unthumbprinted and duplicated entries [Para 50][Para 52][Para 73]. The findings and the reasoning repeat the same thumbprint analysis verbatim in two places [Para 51][Para 72]. The conciliation report is dated both 15 May 2024 and 15 April 2024 [Para 6][Para 43]. One paragraph garbles the number of unsettled points as "42" and another as "2" [Para 21]. Representative and delegate names survive only as initials and fragmented Khmer syllables, which is why they are not reproduced here [Para 42][Para 49].
