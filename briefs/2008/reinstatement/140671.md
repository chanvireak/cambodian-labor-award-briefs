# Award No. 137/07 — MPA Security Services International Ltd (Arbitration Council, 10 January 2008)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators: Ing Sothy, An Nan, Kong Phallack (Chair) · Doc ID 140671

## 1. Facts
MPA Security Services International Ltd, at Wat Bo Village, Salakomreuk Commune, Siem Reap, provides security services and employs around 2,000 workers [Para 24]. The claimants were the Cambodian Tourism and Service Workers Federation (CYSWF) and the MPA Security Workers Trade Union at Siem Reap International Airport, whose registration the Department of Labour Disputes refused by letter No. 766 dated 1 August 2007 because the three leaders were said to be ineligible, the president having been born in 1983 and so under the age of 25 required by Article 269 of the Labour Law [Para 26][Para 29]. The company dismissed 17 workers — leaders, advisers and members — 12 on 3 August 2007 and five on 8 August 2007; none had a written contract [Para 31][Para 69]. The trigger was a direction of 18 July 2007 transferring them from Siem Reap airport to Phnom Penh airport or to other Siem Reap clients, which they refused, saying the posts were far from their homes in Siem Reap and Kompong Thom and unsuited to their x-ray and airport skills [Para 40][Para 46][Para 85]. They demanded reinstatement, alleging union discrimination; the company said the airport no longer wanted them [Para 12][Para 35].

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. (Issue 1) Whether the dismissal of the 17 workers was due to union discrimination? [Para 68]
2. (Issue 1, second question) Whether the dismissal of the 17 workers was in compliance with the law? [Para 74]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** The dismissals came after the company was notified of the union's establishment, so they were acts of union discrimination; the workers had no homes in Phnom Penh, were trained only in airport and x-ray work, and the transfer to unrelated clients did not match their skills, so they asked to return to Siem Reap International Airport [Para 50][Para 46][Para 49].

**Employer (respondent):** Siem Reap International Airport no longer required the 17 workers after their strike and petition supporting a dismissed colleague caused chaos there; they breached the agreement of 12 June 2007 and the direction of 18 July 2007, refused work at the alternative sites and so were dismissed for misconduct, not for union activity [Para 37][Para 38][Para 51].

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** granted — the employer was ordered to reinstate the 17 workers, assign them to locations appropriate to their skills and pay back wages from the date they were officially dismissed [Para 102]

On discrimination, the panel noted that the absence of written contracts made the workers' engagements contracts of unspecified duration under Article 67(7) of the Labour Law [Para 69]. The worker party bears the burden of proving union discrimination, as held in Awards 90/06 — Evergreen, 112/06 — River Rich and 01/07 — Supreme, and here it produced no testimony or evidence beyond the timing of the dismissals; the panel therefore found insufficient evidence of discrimination [Para 70][Para 71][Para 73].

On legality, the company relied on Article 83(B)(3) (serious infractions of disciplinary regulations) but the panel found the Internal Work Rules nowhere classify disobedience as serious misconduct and provide for verbal and written warnings first [Para 77][Para 79][Para 80][Para 91]. Under Article 2(2), and following Awards 17/03 and 18/03, the employer may manage and transfer workers only if wages are not cut, the new location is not far away, shifts are not reversed and the work does not require a substantially different skill set; transferring x-ray staff from Siem Reap to Phnom Penh and to hotels failed those conditions, so the workers could refuse the management direction [Para 83][Para 84][Para 86]. The panel still found the workers at fault for refusing without negotiating, since security could be affected [Para 87][Para 88].

That fault did not save the dismissals: Article 27 requires sanctions proportional to the misconduct, and dismissal was disproportionate to a transfer the employer had mishandled [Para 89][Para 90]. Article 26 bars disciplinary action for conduct known for more than fifteen days and deems the right to dismiss for serious misconduct renounced if not exercised within seven days; the company knew of the refusal on 18 July 2007 but dismissed only on 3 and 8 August 2007, so it had renounced the right and the dismissals were unlawful [Para 91][Para 92][Para 95][Para 96]. Reinstatement followed, but the panel held the airport is not the employer and the company may assign the workers elsewhere provided it trains them for skills they lack [Para 98][Para 99][Para 100][Para 101].

## 5. Ratio decidendi
A dismissal for refusing an unlawful transfer is itself unlawful where it comes more than seven days after the employer learned of the refusal, because Article 26 of the Labour Law deems the disciplinary right renounced; and the sanction must in any event be proportionate under Article 27 [Para 92][Para 95][Para 90]. A transfer is lawful only if wages are unchanged, the destination is not far, shifts are not reversed and the skills required are not substantially different, and an employer that disregards those conditions cannot treat the resulting refusal as serious misconduct [Para 84][Para 86].

**Obiter dicta:** The panel observed that the workers should have negotiated before refusing, and that their absence could have compromised client security and property [Para 87][Para 88].

## 6. Source defects disclosed
The union claimant is named inconsistently: the header gives "Cambodian Tourism and Service Workers Federation (CYSWF)", while the facts call it the "Cambodian Tourism Trade Union Federation" and later "CTSWF" [Para 4][Para 25][Para 52]. The secretary's name is spelled two different ways in the narrative and in the dismissal table [Para 28][Para 33]. The facts say the workers were "officially dismissed" on 3 and 8 August 2007, but the procedural history dates the dismissal to 18 July 2007 [Para 14][Para 31]. The two dismissal tables are badly scrambled by OCR — names, ID numbers, employment dates and positions are interleaved, one member's ID is printed twice, and one adviser's row lacks a name — so the full list of 17 cannot be reconstructed reliably [Para 32][Para 34]. The parties' representatives list is also cut across lines, leaving roles detached from names [Para 11].
