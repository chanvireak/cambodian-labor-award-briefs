# Fa Ying Da Gong (Cambodia) Garment (ក្រុមហ៊ុន កាត់ដេរ ហ្វា យ៉ីង ដ្រា ហ្កុង (ខេមបូឌៀរ) ហ្គាម៉ែន) v. C.CAWDU — Case 47/06
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia, Phnom Penh; award dated 3 July 2006, issued under Article 313 of the Labour Law [Para 1].

## 1. Facts

The employer was Fa Ying Da Gong (Cambodia) Garment (ក្រុមហ៊ុន កាត់ដេរ ហ្វា យ៉ីង ដ្រា ហ្កុង (ខេមបូឌៀរ) ហ្គាម៉ែន), located on Chom Chao road, Stung Meanchey commune, Meanchey district, Phnom Penh, employing about 2,500 workers at two factories (Factory 2 and Factory 3; Factory 1 had been closed) [Paras 1, 21]. The worker party was the Coalition of Cambodian Apparel Workers' Democratic Union (C.CAWDU, សម្ព័ន្ធ សហជីព ប្រជាធិបតេយ្យ កម្មករ កាត់ដេរ កម្ពុជា) [Para 1]. The panel comprised Seng Vouchhun, arbitrator chosen by the employer; Tuon Siphan, arbitrator chosen by the workers; and Nhan Somuninth, president chosen by the two arbitrators [Para 1].

The worker, Ms. P, was hired in 1999 to cut garment tags and was paid by output [Paras 27, 32]. The company classified her as a "contractor" (កម្មករ ម៉ៅការ) or "floating worker" (កម្មករ បណ្តែត) and did not provide her with a written contract, seniority pay, attendance bonus, annual leave, or severance on dismissal [Para 32]. She worked regular hours, generally eight hours a day, sometimes two to three hours of overtime daily, and sometimes on Sundays or public holidays [Para 28]. She was a member of C.CAWDU and joined a strike at Factory 3 from 22 February to 13 March 2006 [Para 33]. After the 13 March 2006 agreement ended the strike, the workers returned to work and were paid [Para 34]. The company dismissed Ms. P on 18 March 2006, saying she was a "contractor" and had performed poorly [Paras 27, 40, 98].

Conciliation on 31 May 2006 settled one point: the company agreed to reinstate Mr. P [Para 10]. The remaining point—reinstatement of Ms. P—was not settled and went to arbitration [Para 11]. The non-conciliation report No. 800 K.B.V/A.K./V.K. was dated 8 June 2006 and reached the Arbitration Council on 9 June 2006 [Para 5]. The hearing was held on 16 June 2006 [Para 7]. The company maintained at the hearing that Ms. P had no employment contract and could be dismissed at any time [Para 40].

## 2. Issues in dispute

1. Whether the company had an employment contract with Ms. P? [Para 40]
2. Whether that contract was for a fixed term or an indefinite term? [Para 52]
3. Whether Ms. P was a permanent or a casual worker, and what rights she had under the Labour Law? [Para 57]
4. Whether her dismissal was lawful? [Para 93]

## 3. Positions of the parties

**Workers (claimant):** Ms. P had worked for the company since 1999 as a tag cutter, regularly eight hours a day with overtime, and was a union member. The company's label of "contractor" was a device to avoid labour-law obligations. She was entitled to reinstatement under the 13 March 2006 agreement and the Labour Law.

**Employer (respondent):** The company treated Ms. P as a "contractor" (កម្មករ ម៉ៅការ) with no employment contract, so it had no labour-law obligations to her and could dismiss her at any time without following any procedure [Para 40]. It also said at the hearing that she had performed poorly [Paras 98, 101].

## 4. Award and reasoning

The panel applied Article 1 of the Labour Law, which governs all employment relationships in Cambodia [Para 41], and Article 3, which defines an employee as a person who contracts to work for remuneration under the direction and control of another [Para 42]. It held that a labour contract may be oral and need not be in writing; Article 65 allows an oral contract, and Article 66 treats an oral contract as one with the terms of the internal regulations [Paras 44–46]. It found Ms. P was an employee because she worked under the company's direction through a team leader who was the company's representative, and the company retained the right to control or dismiss her [Paras 49–50].

On the contract type, the panel applied Articles 66 and 67, which distinguish fixed-term from indefinite-term contracts, and found that because no written contract with an end date existed, the contract was indefinite [Paras 52–56]. It rejected the "contractor" label, applying Article 45's definition of a contractor as an independent businessperson who hires others for a lump sum and does not work under the other party's direction [Paras 73–76]. It held that a piece-rate worker is not necessarily a contractor and that the true nature of the relationship, not the label, determines status [Paras 80–88].

On worker category, the panel applied Article 9 and found Ms. P was a permanent worker because she performed regular work of a permanent character for over six years [Paras 57–58, 86]. It noted that even a casual worker has the same rights as a permanent worker under Article 10 [Para 90], and that claims are limited to three years by Article 120 [Paras 91–92].

On dismissal, the panel applied Article 74, which requires a valid reason related to the worker's aptitude or conduct or the operation of the enterprise [Paras 93–95]. It found the company had no valid reason: she had worked for over six years without disciplinary breach, and the company's claim of poor performance was not credible [Paras 98–99]. The dismissal also breached clauses 1 and 6 of the 13 March 2006 agreement [Para 103].

**Outcome:** granted — the company must reinstate Ms. P as a permanent employee with an indefinite-term contract, pay her wages and benefits for the three years before the award, and pay back wages at the minimum wage from the date of dismissal until reinstatement, within one month of the award taking effect [Paras 104–106].

## 5. Ratio decidendi

A worker's status is determined by the substance of the relationship, not the label the employer attaches: a person who works regularly under the employer's direction and control is an employee under Article 3 of the Labour Law, even if called a "contractor" or "floating worker" [Paras 42, 49, 87]. An oral employment contract is valid and, absent a written end date, is an indefinite-term contract [Paras 44–46, 56]. Regular work of a permanent character makes the worker permanent under Article 9 [Paras 57–58, 86]. A dismissal without a valid reason under Article 74 is unlawful and entitles the worker to reinstatement with back pay [Paras 95, 102, 104–106].
