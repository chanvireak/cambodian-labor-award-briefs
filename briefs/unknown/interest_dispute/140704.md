# Case 44/06 – Gold Fame (ក្រុមហ៊ុន កាត់ដេរ ហ្គោល ហ្វេម)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia, Phnom Penh — arbitral award under Article 313 of the Labour Law, dated 22 June 2006.

## 1. Facts

The employer was Krong Ham Kattdel Gold Fame (ក្រុមហ៊ុន កាត់ដេរ ហ្គោល ហ្វេម), a garment factory at Kampong Pring village, Setbo commune, Saang district, Kandal province [Para 1]. The worker side was the Coalition of Cambodian Apparel Workers' Democratic Union (C.CAWDU) and the Cambodian Workers' Union Federation [Para 2]. The arbitration panel comprised arbitrator Hem Honarith chosen by the employer, arbitrator Tun Siphan chosen by the workers, and president Ang Eng Thong chosen by the two arbitrators [Para 1].

On 28 April 2006, 124 workers from the factory petitioned C.CAWDU to help resolve their labour dispute [Para 47]. On 26 May 2006 the Kandal provincial Department of Labour and Vocational Training conciliated 54 demands raised by four union federations [Para 14]. After conciliation the meeting agreed to negotiate only 17 points, settling 9 and leaving 8 unsettled [Para 15]. The non-conciliation report No. 084 K.B.V/Kandal, dated 30 May 2006, was sent to the Arbitration Council Secretariat the same day [Para 12].

The hearing was held on 9 June 2006 from 14:00 to 18:30 at the Phnom Penh Center [Para 14]. The employer did not attend, claiming it had already signed an agreement with the workers covering all disputed points [Para 1]. On 31 May 2006 C.CAWDU notified the company of a union election and listed 31 candidates [Para 38]. On 5 June 2006 persons claiming to represent the federations signed an agreement with the employer settling the disputes [Para 39]. On 5 June 2006 Kong Adit, a C.CAWDU representative, wrote asking the Arbitration Council to continue case 44/06 [Para 40]. The employer sent letters on 5 and 7 June 2006 asking that the case be closed without a hearing [Para 45] [Para 46]. At the hearing the workers said 17 of the 124 signatories had taken money and resigned, and the union had not known of the agreement [Para 52].

## 2. Issues in dispute

The eight unsettled demands before the panel were:

1. (Issue 1) Whether the company must raise piece rates in all sections? [Para 3]
2. (Issue 2) Whether the ironing-section rate for the easiest shirts must be raised to USD 0.45 per dozen? [Para 4] [Para 118]
3. (Issue 3) Whether the pressing and checking sections must be paid by output, per dozen? [Para 4] [Para 121]
4. (Issue 4) Whether the employer must pay the 10,100 riel health-check fee immediately? [Para 5] [Para 132]
5. (Issue 5) Whether the employer may deduct money when a worker loses or replaces an ID card? [Para 6] [Para 139]
6. (Issue 6) Whether the employer must abolish "floating" (casual) worker status and make workers with over one month of service permanent? [Para 7] [Para 150]

The panel also had to decide whether the 5 June 2006 agreement bound the 107 remaining claimants and whether it could proceed with the employer absent [Para 88] [Para 100].

## 3. Positions of the parties

**Workers (claimant):** They worked eight hours plus four hours of overtime a day but received only USD 50 a month, sometimes less than USD 45 [Para 60]. Working only eight hours a day would not earn them the base wage [Para 61]. They claimed the piece rate set by the employer did not allow an average worker to reach the USD 45 minimum wage [Para 114]. For the pressing and checking sections they said other sections already worked by output and that the employer set a target of 30 shirts per hour for about 1,000 workers, punishing those who missed it [Para 72] [Para 73].

The workers asked for the 10,100 riel health-check payment immediately [Para 74]; the employer refused immediate payment but agreed to pay on termination of the employment contract by either side [Para 74] [Para 133]. On ID cards, the workers said the employer required the cards, so a lost card should be charged at actual market price, but a replacement card should carry no charge because the worker was not at fault [Para 75] [Para 140]. On floating workers, they said about half the workforce was casual and denied annual leave [Para 78].

The employer did not appear at the hearing, relying on the validity of the 5 June 2006 agreement [Para 1] [Para 58]. It argued the agreement was valid and binding and asked that the case be closed without a hearing [Para 45] [Para 46].

## 4. Award and reasoning

The panel held that the employer's unilateral refusal to attend, based on the agreement's validity, was not a proper reason for absenting itself, and it continued the proceedings [Para 88] [Para 89]. It found that the 5 June 2006 agreement did not bind the 107 workers who had not signed it, because the 17 signatories were merely election candidates who had not yet been elected and had no legal authority to represent the workers [Para 93] [Para 94] [Para 100]. The agreement bound only those who signed it [Para 101]. The panel therefore defined the worker party as the 107 original claimants [Para 108].

On demand 1, the panel found the claim concerned the employer's power to set piece rates under Article 108 of the Labour Law and the minimum wage guarantee [Para 110] [Para 113]. It dismissed the claim because the workers produced insufficient evidence identifying which workers failed to receive the USD 45 minimum wage [Para 115] [Para 116]. It dismissed demand 2 for the same evidential reason [Para 119] [Para 120]. It dismissed demand 3 as a demand beyond statutory rights, an interests dispute, and because no union with the highest representative status represented the workers [Para 128] [Para 130] [Para 131].

On demand 4, the panel applied Article 247 of the Labour Law and prior awards holding that the employer must pay the pre-employment health-check fee [Para 134]. It ordered the employer to pay 10,100 riel to each of the 107 workers who had not yet been paid, within three weeks of the award taking effect [Para 172] [Para 173]. On demand 5, it held the employer may charge only the actual market price of a card the worker lost, and may not charge for a replacement made for a reasonable cause [Para 146] [Para 149] [Para 174] [Para 175]. On demand 6, it refused the demand to abolish floating-worker status [Para 154], but held that workers averaging at least 21 days a month over two consecutive months must be treated as permanent, and ordered that two workers be reclassified [Para 166] [Para 167] [Para 177].

**Outcome:** partly granted.

## 5. Ratio decidendi

An arbitration agreement signed by workers who are only candidates in an uncompleted union election does not bind the workers they claim to represent; only signatories are bound [Para 94] [Para 100] [Para 101]. A demand that the employer extend a piece-rate system is an interests dispute, and the panel will not arbitrate it where no union with highest representative status represents the workers [Para 128] [Para 130] [Para 131]. Evidential gaps defeat wage claims: a claim that piece rates prevent the minimum wage fails without proof of which workers fell below USD 45 [Para 115] [Para 116]. The employer's statutory duty to pay pre-employment health checks under Article 247 is enforceable by award, and the employer's administrative right to manage the enterprise does not extend to charging workers for reasonable card replacement [Para 134] [Para 146] [Para 149]. Workers who in fact work permanent schedules are permanent employees under Article 9 and the 21-day rule, whatever label the employer uses [Para 166] [Para 167].

## 6. Source defects disclosed

The source text is a Khmer-language award with OCR corruption: several paragraphs contain garbled fragments and stray characters, including the employer-name line and the article references [Para 1] [Para 110] [Para 122]. The text also shows an internal inconsistency in the date of the 5 June agreement, which is given as 5 May 2006 in two places [Para 39] [Para 40]. Some demand numbering is duplicated or misaligned in the source, and the operative section does not expressly address demands 7 and 8 [Para 8] [Para 9] [Para 168]. These defects are noted; the substantive findings remain legible.
