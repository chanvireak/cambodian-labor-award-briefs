# Award No. 48/13 — Sumi (Cambodia) Wiring Systems Co., Ltd (Arbitration Council, 18 April 2013)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators — Mr Y Soun Ty (chosen by the employer), Mr T Si Phan (chosen by the workers), chair Mr Pe Bun Chea · Doc ID 139666

## 1. Facts
Sumi (Cambodia) Wiring Systems Co., Ltd, a car wiring-harness factory on National Road 4, Pur Senchey district, Phnom Penh, employed about 858 workers per the conciliation report, 759 per the employer. [Para 71][Para 73][Para 74] The Cambodia Asia union at the factory, registered under certificate No. 2748 of 14 January 2013 with Mr M as president and Mr P as vice-president, was not the most representative union and brought 11 demands; conciliation on 20 February 2013 failed because the employer did not attend, two points settled at hearing, one was withdrawn, and eight remained. [Para 76][Para 77][Para 22][Para 28] Both parties chose a non-binding award. [Para 29]

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. Whether the employer must raise the base wage for each operator from US$61 to US$80 a month, an increase of US$19? [Para 78]
2. Whether the employer must immediately stop compelling workers to work overtime? [Para 103][Para 224]
3. Whether the employer discriminates against the union — by refusing to recognise it, by warning and dismissing Mr P and Mr M, and by collecting thumbprints against the union on 12 March 2013? [Para 109][Para 236]
4. Whether the employer must stop deducting the additional seniority bonus when a worker takes first-aid (បឋម) treatment leave inside the factory? [Para 134][Para 274]
5. Whether the employer must pay a US$10 monthly good-work bonus to every worker in all sections? [Para 137][Para 286]
6. Whether the employer must stop moving public-holiday rest days to other dates? [Para 148][Para 303]
7. Whether the employer must let workers leave the compound at lunch without following the notification procedure? [Para 158][Para 315]
8. Whether the employer must take back Mr M, president of the Cambodia Asia union at the factory, and let him work normally again? [Para 168][Para 326]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** Goods cost more, Article 104 of the Labour Law requires a wage supporting a decent standard of living, and US$80 a month is enough to live on; operators are skilled, not ordinary garment workers, having to pass a test and train for four months plus three months' probation. [Para 81][Para 84][Para 88] The employer compelled overtime and warned those who refused, withheld the union's registration notice, and dismissed the two leaders for their union role. [Para 107][Para 122]
**Employer (respondent):** No law, collective agreement or agreement obliges it to pay US$80; the work is hi-tech and simple, most operators get a US$15 J-star allowance, and it will follow any Ministry minimum-wage decision. [Para 93][Para 97][Para 100] Overtime is voluntary — staff record only volunteers — and the warnings and dismissal followed the two men's own misconduct; it knew nothing of the union leadership until the first hearing on 7 March 2013. [Para 227][Para 264][Para 269]

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** partly granted — the employer is ordered to apply the voluntary principle to overtime and to take Mr M back into work; the wage increase, discrimination claim, seniority-bonus deduction, US$10 bonus, holiday-calendar change and lunch-break exit claim are refused. The award is non-binding until no party objects in writing within 08 days. [Para 369][Para 370][Para 375][Para 376]

On issue 1 the panel found no provision of the Labour Law, no collective agreement and no agreement fixing a US$80 base wage, so the demand was an interest dispute. [Para 210][Para 211] Because an interest award replaces the collective agreement for a year and removes other workers' right to strike on that subject, it held it could decide such a dispute only where the claimant union is representative or several unions together represent over half the workforce; this union was not, so the claim was refused. [Para 217][Para 218][Para 223]

On issue 3 the panel applied Article 4 of Prakas No. 80 of 1 March 1999: overtime rests on voluntariness and the employer may not compel or discipline a worker who declines. [Para 224][Para 226] Notification and sign-up were not coercive, nor was questioning the C & C team, but warning Mr M for declining overtime contradicted the spirit of the Prakas, and the voluntary principle was ordered applied. [Para 231][Para 234][Para 235]

On issue 4 the panel found no discrimination: security staff had signed for the union's notice, so the employer could not deny knowledge, but it accepted the union and did not obstruct membership; the warnings rested on misconduct the workers did not deny; and the thumbprint exercise was document administration, not coercion. [Para 257][Para 266][Para 271]

On issues 5, 6 and 7 the claims either had no present object — the seniority deduction had never been applied — or concerned the employer's lawful managerial discretion under Article 2 of the Labour Law: the US$10 payment was a licence allowance tied to a QA examination; the 8 January 2013 calendar-change agreement, signed by section representatives, covered the holiday shifts; and the lunch-break exit procedure was justified by food-poisoning cases, traffic and product-loss risks. [Para 280][Para 298][Para 304][Para 319][Para 324]

On issue 11 the panel held that Mr M, engaged on 3 January 2012 with no written contract, held a contract of undetermined duration under Article 67 paragraph 1 and Article 76 paragraph 7, so his termination was a dismissal to which union-leader protection could attach. [Para 335][Para 337][Para 338] The three conditions were met — protected leader under Article 4 of Prakas No. 305, dismissed on 13 February 2013 within the 30-day protection period, notice given — and the employer dismissed him without the labour inspector's prior authorisation, notifying only afterwards. [Para 350][Para 352][Para 365] Reinstatement was ordered. [Para 367]

Mr Y Soun Ty dissented on issue 11: because the union never notified the employer of its local leadership by all available means, Mr M never acquired Article 293 protection, and warnings for serious misconduct justified dismissal. [Para 395][Para 397]

## 5. Ratio decidendi
An interest dispute over wages can be arbitrated only where the claimant union is the most representative in the enterprise or the unions together represent more than half the workers, since an interest award displaces the collective agreement and suspends others' right to strike. Overtime is voluntary, and a warning for declining it is unlawful. A union leader engaged without a written contract holds an undetermined-duration contract, and dismissal within the protected period without the labour inspector's prior consent is unlawful whatever misconduct is alleged. [Para 218][Para 234][Para 338][Para 363]

**Obiter dicta:** Joining or leaving a union is the worker's own freedom, and a union leader should not be casual about identifying the local leadership to the employer even where the employer's document-receipt procedure is deficient. [Para 262][Para 392]

## 6. Source defects disclosed
The workforce figure is printed twice, 858 and 759. [Para 73][Para 74] The summary states 11 demands, 2 settled, 1 withdrawn, 08 left, but numbers the settled points 2 and 5 and the withdrawn one 6. [Para 26][Para 28] The claimant's name is written inconsistently (Mon Sokhnang, Mom Sokhnang) and once garbled as "Hun Sen Sokhnang" in the framing of issue 11; the panel adopted one spelling, redacted here as Mr M. [Para 170][Para 172][Para 326] The dissent at paras 389–394 reaches the opposite conclusion on the third protection condition from the majority at paras 355–356; both are reproduced as printed. [Para 355][Para 394] The signature block garbles the chair's name beside the printed "Pen Bun Chea", and OCR debris ("CAZA", "CHZ", "QRZ", "2017") survives throughout. [Para 377]
