# Award No. 226/13 — Pou Yuen (Cambodia) (Arbitration Council, 28 November 2013)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators (Ing Sothy, employer-appointed; An Nan, worker-appointed; Kong Phallack, chair) · Doc ID 139884 · unofficial English translation

## 1. Facts
Pou Yuen (Cambodia) Enterprise, a footwear manufacturer at Sang Thmey Road, Khan Sen Sok, Phnom Penh, employs 3,500 workers [Para 23][Para 24]. Three unions — the local union of the Workers Union Federation (WUF), the Union of Workers' Will at Pou Yuen and the Union of Workers Spirit at Pou Yuen — are joint claimants; the parties agree their membership is 1,900, measured by the wage deductions for union dues made in October 2013 [Para 24][Para 52]. The Department of Labour Disputes received the WUF complaint on 16 September 2013; conciliation on 14 October 2013 resolved four of eight issues, and non-conciliation report No. 1297 went to the Secretariat on 18 October 2013 [Para 6][Para 7][Para 8]. At the hearing of 8 November 2013 the workers withdrew issues 1 and 4, leaving issues 2 and 3 [Para 15][Para 16]. The award issued on 28 November 2013, deferred by agreement from 13 November [Para 20].

Because the parties are signatories to the Memorandum of Understanding on Improving Industrial Relations in the Garment Industry of 3 October 2012, rights disputes go to binding arbitration while interest disputes are arbitrated non-bindingly [Para 17][Para 18][Para 19].

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. Whether the employer must use the average of the wages the workers earned over the last 12 months — rather than base wage alone — as the basis for payment during annual leave and payment in lieu of remaining annual leave? [Para 24][Para 25][Para 26]
2. Whether the employer must provide a 20 US dollar gift to workers who are about to take maternity leave? [Para 30][Para 31][Para 32]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** annual leave is paid leave and a lawful entitlement, so payment must be proportionate to wages already earned; using base wage omits the attendance bonus, the accommodation and transport allowance and the incentive bonus [Para 27][Para 28]. They also argue that if the employer took base wage plus perquisites as the basis for docking pay for unpaid leave, they would not accept docking on base wage alone [Para 29]. On maternity, they say the women have worked hard for many years, so a 20 US dollar gift on departure is warranted [Para 31][Para 55].
**Employer (respondent):** it has calculated on base wage since it began operating and also docks unpaid leave on base wage [Para 28][Para 29]. It says it cannot afford the 20 US dollar gift; normally two or three workers a month take maternity leave but 50 did in the latest month, and no agreement or internal rule supports the demand [Para 31][Para 32].

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** issue 2 granted as a binding rights award; issue 3 rejected as a non-binding interest award [Para 57][Para 58].

*Issue 2 (annual-leave calculation).* The panel held this a rights dispute because it concerns the interpretation and enforcement of Article 166 and Article 168 of the Labour Law [Para 40]. Article 168 requires an allowance at least equal to the average wage, bonuses, benefits and indemnities, including benefits in kind but excluding expense reimbursement, earned in the twelve months before departure [Para 39]. Following a line of earlier awards, the panel ordered the employer to divide the total wages earned in the last 12 months by 12 and then by 26, and multiply by the days of leave taken or remaining [Para 41][Para 42][Para 57]. The employer's base-wage method therefore breached Article 168 [Para 42].
*Issue 3 (maternity gift).* No law, agreement, collective agreement, internal rule or past practice obliges a 20 US dollar gift, so this was an interest dispute [Para 43][Para 44]. On standing, the panel applied Article 96(2) of the Labour Law and Clause 9 of Prakas No. 305 of 22 November 2001: an interest dispute may be brought by the union with most representative status, or by a group of unions whose members are a majority of the workforce [Para 45][Para 46][Para 48][Para 49][Para 50]. The three unions held 1,900 of 3,500 workers, or 54.29 per cent, and so had standing [Para 52][Para 53][Para 54]. On the merits, the only reason offered was long service, with no specific basis in law or practice, so the demand was rejected in equity [Para 55][Para 56][Para 58].

## 5. Ratio decidendi
Payment during annual leave and in lieu of remaining leave must be computed on the average of wages, bonuses, benefits and indemnities in kind earned over the preceding twelve months, divided by 12 and by 26, and not on base wage alone; a base-wage computation breaches Article 168 of the Labour Law and is a rights dispute enforceable as a binding award [Para 39][Para 41][Para 42][Para 57]. Where no law, agreement, collective agreement, internal rule or past practice supports a demand, it is an interest dispute, and the claimant must show most representative status or a majority coalition of unions to be heard [Para 43][Para 48][Para 54].

**Obiter dicta:** the panel reasoned that an interest award takes the place of a collective agreement for one year and removes the right to strike on that subject even for non-members, which is why majority standing is required [Para 46][Para 47]. Arbitrator Ing Sothy dissented on issue 2, arguing that the calculation must track paragraph 2 of Article 167 and Article 168 exactly and that any different methodology is illegitimate unless the parties agree [Para 59][Para 60].
