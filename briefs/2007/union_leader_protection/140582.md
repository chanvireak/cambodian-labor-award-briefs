# Award No. 09/07 — Sok Prim Garment PTY LTD (សុខ ព្រីម ហ្គាមេន ភីធី អ៉ី អិលធីឌី) (Arbitration Council, 12 February 2007)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators — employer-appointed អ៊ុក វ៉ា, worker-appointed អន ណន, president អេង ថុង · Doc ID 140582

## 1. Facts
The Solidarity Union Federation and the Solidarity Union at the Sok Prim Garment factory (សហព័ន្ធ សហជីព សាមគ្គីភាព និង សហជីព សាមគ្គីភាព ប្រចាំ រោងចក្រ សុខ ព្រីម) brought a collective dispute against Sok Prim Garment PTY LTD (សុខ ព្រីម ហ្គាមេន ភីធី អ៉ី អិលធីឌី), a Kandal-province garment employer of about 1,100 workers with three factory unions [Para 1][Para 12]. In December 2006 the company applied to the labour inspector for a two-month suspension of work from 28 December 2006 to 28 February 2007 for 300 workers, and in fact suspended 120 of them from 28 December 2006 [Para 13][Para 14]. It found work from other factories after about ten days and called the workers back, more than 90% returning [Para 15][Para 16]. The union claimed USD 20 per worker per month for accommodation during the suspension; the company offered USD 6 and the single point went unsettled to the Council [Para 2][Para 8].

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. (Issue 1) Whether the employer discriminated against the union or against pregnant women when it suspended work? [Para 24]
2. (Issue 2) Whether the employer was under a legal duty to provide accommodation to the workers during the period of suspension? [Para 33]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant union):** The suspension was selective — it hit union members and pregnant workers — and the three unions demanded USD 20 a month per worker for the two months named in the suspension application, even though the factory actually stopped work for only about ten days [Para 18][Para 19][Para 24].

**Employer (respondent):** The company genuinely had no work, suspended broadly without regard to union membership, did not suspend any of the three unions' leaders, and did not suspend every pregnant worker; it nevertheless accepted that some union members and some pregnant women were among those affected [Para 20][Para 22][Para 23][Para 25].

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** dismissed — the panel rejected the claim for USD 20 a month per worker for the two months of suspension [Para 50].

On Issue 1 the panel applied its earlier awards No. 79/05 and No. 99/06 for the rule that the worker side bears the burden of proving anti-union discrimination [Para 26]. It found no evidence supporting the allegation: the suspension was applied across the board regardless of union affiliation, none of the three union leaders was suspended, and other pregnant women in the factory continued to work [Para 27][Para 28][Para 29][Para 30]. It therefore held that the suspension was not based on anti-union or anti-pregnancy discrimination [Para 31].

On Issue 2 the panel applied Article 72(1) of the Labour Law, under which suspension of the contract removes the two principal obligations — the worker's duty to work and the employer's duty to pay wages — unless a contrary provision applies [Para 33]. It read the words "continues as before" in that article as preserving non-wage obligations, including any duty to provide accommodation that the employer owed during employment [Para 34][Para 35][Para 36]. That duty therefore survives a suspension only where the employer was already bound to house the workers; where it provided no housing and had no such policy, nothing continues [Para 37][Para 47].

The panel distinguished Article 204 of the Labour Law, which entitles permanently resident agricultural workers to free employer-provided housing, and found no equivalent rule for the garment sector [Para 38][Para 39]. It recorded that this employer housed no workers, that there was no collective agreement or company policy on housing, and that a USD 12 monthly payment made during a 2004 suspension was a one-off negotiated outcome rather than established practice [Para 40][Para 41][Para 44][Para 45]. It noted that the parties agreed the sector norm on suspension is USD 5 to USD 7 a month where the labour inspector has properly approved the suspension, a norm they remain free to bargain over [Para 46][Para 47].

## 5. Ratio decidendi
Suspension of a labour contract under Article 72(1) extinguishes the wage obligation, and an employer's duty to provide accommodation during suspension subsists only if the employer was already obliged by law, collective agreement or settled practice to house the workers while they worked; a single ex gratia payment made during an earlier suspension does not create that obligation [Para 33][Para 37][Para 44].

**Obiter dicta:** The panel observed that discrimination during a suspension, even if proved, may found a claim for other compensation but does not convert the suspension into an entitlement to accommodation pay [Para 32].

## 6. Source defects disclosed
The case number is printed with mixed Khmer and Arabic digits [Para 1]. The employer's name appears as Sok Prim in some places and with different initial consonants and a truncated final word in others [Para 12][Para 33]. The amount claimed is stated three ways: USD 20 a month in the conciliation record, USD 400 for two months in the reasons, and USD 20 a month again in the operative order [Para 2][Para 24][Para 50]. The number of affected workers moves from 300 in the application to 120 actually suspended [Para 13]. The company's offer is recorded as USD 6 in one place and the sector range as USD 5 to USD 7 in another, without reconciliation [Para 2][Para 17]. The presiding arbitrator's name is printed in the header and again, differently and partly garbled, in the signature block [Para 1][Para 52].
