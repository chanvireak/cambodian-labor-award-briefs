# Award No. 79/11 — Lim Hieng Yu Enterprise (Arbitration Council, 8 August 2011)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators (Oeng Sothy for the employer party, An Nan for the worker party, Pen Bun Chhea chair) · Doc ID 140046

## 1. Facts
The respondent is Lim Hieng Yu Enterprise Private, a family-run cake and confectionery company in Kampong Speu registered in 2009, employing about 100 workers; the claimant is the Cambodia Food and Service Workers' Union Federation, acting for the newly formed Democratic Workers Union of the enterprise. [Para 2][Para 86][Para 87][Para 88][Para 89] That union was elected on 29 May 2011, notified the employer on 8 June, and applied for registration on 5 June; the Labour Disputes Department refused registration on 21 June 2011 and the union was still unregistered when the award issued. [Para 90][Para 91][Para 93][Para 94] The union gave strike notice on 28 June 2011 over five demands; conciliation on 27 June failed on all five, three were settled at the first hearing, and the case proceeded on two: reinstatement of eight dismissed workers and the working-hours/wage demands. [Para 13][Para 15][Para 18][Para 19]

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. Whether the enterprise must take back the eight workers it is said to have dismissed, and failing that pay them termination compensation? [Para 98][Para 171]
2. Whether the enterprise must stop imposing more than eight hours' work a day, pay overtime at the statutory premium, pay night work at the statutory premium, and pay wages equal to the garment-sector minimum wage? [Para 154][Para 216]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant, through the federation):** they never resigned; the employer called them in, asked for identity cards, and made them thumbprint resignation letters, warning that wages would not be paid otherwise; if reinstatement is refused they are owed statutory termination compensation, and wages must at least match the garment minimum wage. [Para 100][Para 160][Para 182][Para 183][Para 223]
**Employer (respondent):** every worker signed or thumbprinted a resignation letter and was paid final wages for days worked; replacements have already been hired, so reinstatement is impossible; the enterprise is a small family business that can pay only USD 40 a month for an eight-hour day, though it accepts the eight-hour day and voluntary overtime. [Para 101][Para 102][Para 164][Para 173][Para 222]

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** partly granted — reinstatement refused, but the employer was ordered to pay each of the eight workers termination indemnity of 5% of wages paid during the contract plus payment in lieu of unused annual leave; ordered to apply an eight-hour day; its demand for garment-level minimum wage was refused; and it was ordered to pay overtime at 50% and night work at 130% of the day rate. [Para 250][Para 251][Para 252][Para 253]

On the first issue the panel first held the Labour Law applicable, Article 1 expressly covering handicraft enterprises, so the family character of the business gave no exemption. [Para 168][Para 169][Para 170] It then placed the burden of proving involuntary resignation on the party alleging it, following awards 12/10 Blue Sam Sen Juri and 67/11 Hui Chien. [Para 184][Para 185] Five of the eight had thumbprinted resignation letters; three of those could read and write and had written their own names, and no evidence showed they lacked the will to sign, so their resignations stood; the two who claimed illiteracy had still acknowledged their thumbprints and no coercion was proved; one had simply stopped attending from 11 June 2011 and one never appeared at either hearing. [Para 186][Para 187][Para 189][Para 191][Para 193][Para 197] All eight were therefore found to have resigned voluntarily. [Para 198]

Resignation did not, however, extinguish statutory entitlements: Article 73(6) entitles a worker whose fixed-duration contract ends to indemnity of at least 5% of wages paid during the contract, and the panel followed 65/04 New Phan II in holding that workers who resign of their own accord retain that right. [Para 201][Para 202][Para 203][Para 205] Final wages had already been paid, so nothing was owed on that head, but Articles 166 and 167 required payment for unused annual leave, neither party having shown leave was given. [Para 206][Para 212][Para 213][Para 214]

On the second issue the panel found the enterprise ran 11 to 12 hour days against Article 137's ceiling of eight hours a day and 48 a week, which was unlawful; since the employer conceded an eight-hour day at the hearing, the panel ordered it. [Para 217][Para 218][Para 219][Para 220][Para 221] It refused wage parity with the garment sector: Notification 049/10 of 9 July 2010 fixes USD 61 a month for textile, garment and footwear workers only and does not reach food processing, and under Article 107(2) the guaranteed minimum wage is set by Prakas of the Ministry, so the panel has no jurisdiction to fix it. [Para 225][Para 226][Para 227][Para 232][Para 233][Para 236] It did find the pay practices unlawful for overtime and night work: no overtime premium had been paid, and the flat USD 5 night differential fell short of Article 144's 130%, so it ordered the 50% overtime premium under Article 139 and the 130% night rate. [Para 243][Para 244][Para 245][Para 246]

## 5. Ratio decidendi
A worker who thumbprints a resignation letter and, being literate, writes his or her own name on it is taken to have resigned voluntarily; the worker who alleges the resignation was forced bears the burden of proving it. Voluntary resignation nonetheless preserves the Article 73(6) indemnity of at least 5% of wages paid over the contract and payment in lieu of annual leave under Articles 166–167. [Para 184][Para 188][Para 201][Para 205][Para 213]
An employer may not run daily hours beyond the Article 137 ceiling of eight, and any hours beyond it are voluntary overtime attracting the 50% premium of Article 139, with night work attracting 130% under Article 144. The panel cannot itself award a minimum wage — Article 107(2) reserves that to a Prakas — and Notification 049/10 applies only to the textile, garment and footwear sectors, not to food processing. [Para 217][Para 237][Para 241][Para 226][Para 233]

**Obiter dicta:** although the enterprise had no legal obligation to match the garment minimum wage, the panel observed that living standards across factories were comparable and that the enterprise "should" use the garment minimum wage as the basis for paying its workers on an eight-hour day; that recommendation formed no part of the order. [Para 228][Para 230][Para 231]

## 6. Source defects disclosed
- The findings record "800 workers" for the eight claimants [Para 198], an evident OCR doubling; the operative order correctly lists eight. [Para 250]
- The tally of resignation letters is internally uneven: the panel says five workers thumbprinted, excluding two named workers from that count, then treats one of the eight as never having attended. [Para 186][Para 197]
- The employer-party arbitrator is printed "Oeng Sothy" in the header [Para 1] and differently in the signature block [Para 255]; the chair's name is repeated twice in that block. Both forms are reported, not reconciled.
- Stray fragments break sentences: a bare "year" opens an exhibit entry [Para 70], a "14" intrudes on an exhibit date [Para 47], a "10" intrudes on the employer's eight-hour concession [Para 222], and the Notification 049/10 reasoning is garbled where it says the minimum wage is "in the 40 notification". [Para 228]
- The employer's stated wage floor moves between USD 60 as an average minimum and USD 40 for an eight-hour day within the same submissions. [Para 159][Para 164][Para 222]
- The eight claimants, the employer's director, its lawyer and the union officials are named throughout the source; all are reduced here to initials or roles.
