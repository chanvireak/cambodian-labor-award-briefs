# Award No. 08/11 — PCCS Garments Limited (Arbitration Council, 25 February 2011)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators (Ing Sothy, Ann Vireak, Pen Bunchhea chairing) · Doc ID 139968

## 1. Facts
PCCS Garments Limited is a conglomerate of the SA, GIT, Global and PGL factories; this dispute concerns the PGL factory, where about 550 workers were represented by the Worker Freedom Union Federation (WFUF) and 545 of them held undetermined duration contracts [Para 19][Para 20][Para 59]. Work at PGL stopped section by section — cutting on 17 November 2010, sewing on 28 November 2010, finishing on 5 December 2010 — and between December 2010 and 11 February 2011 the workers had nothing to do while sections were closed, electricity was cut in cutting and sewing, and machines, chairs, tables and computers were trucked out, leaving about 1,229 sewing machines of roughly 3,000 [Para 21][Para 22][Para 56]. During the stoppage the employer paid a US$ 61 main wage plus seniority and a US$ 5 attendance bonus, against average piece-rate earnings of about US$ 120 a month [Para 27][Para 96].

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. Whether the employer must provide work for the workers in the near future? [Para 2][Para 20]
2. Whether the employer must refrain from transporting material out of the factory for outsourcing? [Para 4][Para 51]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** PCCS received purchase orders for December 2010 and January 2011 but diverted them to the other three factories, which were working overtime, in order to cause disorder and force resignations; machines and materials were removed and a voluntary termination policy issued, showing the factory was to close; failure to provide work is a serious offence under Article 83, so the employer must provide work within two months or terminate the contracts and pay dismissal indemnity and damages; some workers were also underpaid because the eye-scanning attendance machine failed to register them [Para 22][Para 34][Para 46][Para 82][Para 99].
**Employer (respondent):** PGL ceased to be the head office when GIT was chosen in March 2011; purchase orders for PGL stopped in December 2010 because buyers withdrew them after frequent strikes and late shipments that forced costly air freight, and it is seeking new buyers though it cannot say when; moving machines was within its managerial right and equipment had always moved between factories, to be returned if PGL obtained orders; it does not intend to dismiss anyone and will continue paying wages and back pay any underpayment [Para 30][Para 31][Para 33][Para 47][Para 58][Para 90].

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** partly granted — within two months of the award the employer must arrange work at the PGL factory, and if it cannot, it must pay damages equivalent to the Article 89 dismissal indemnity, outstanding wages and payment in lieu of unused annual leave when the workers resign; the demand that it stop moving machines and materials is rejected [Para 122][Para 123].
On standing, the panel applied Clause 19 of Prakas No. 99 SKBY, allowing representation by any person expressly authorised in writing, and found the 545 workers had authorised WFUF [Para 60][Para 62]. Issue 1 was classified as a rights dispute because it concerned enforcement of the employment contracts, adjudicated by law under Article 312 paragraph 2 [Para 64][Para 67].
On the merits, the panel found that the employment contracts obliged the employer to provide work and the workers to perform it diligently, but that the employer had in fact no work for PGL [Para 78][Para 79]. Article 83 part A point 5, treating failure to provide sufficient work to a piece-worker as a serious offence, did not apply, because the employer was not withholding work it had but genuinely had none [Para 82][Para 84]. It rejected the demand that the employer be ordered to terminate, since under Article 74 and Award 09/05-Kin Tai an employer may hire, rehire or terminate but cannot be compelled to end a contract, and the employer wished to keep the workforce [Para 85][Para 90][Para 92].
The panel nonetheless held that the absence of work was the main reason pushing the workers to end their contracts, so that they could claim damages equivalent to the Article 89 indemnity under Article 90, which covers cases where the employer's conduct pushes a worker into resigning [Para 100][Para 101][Para 95]. It read Article 89 as 15 days of wage and fringe benefits per year of service, capped at six months, calculated on the average daily wage, and treated wage as actual wages excluding specifically stated allowances, overtime and bonuses [Para 103][Para 105][Para 107]. Clause 34 of Prakas No. 99 SKBY supplied the power to grant such remedies [Para 107][Para 109].
On issue 2, the panel held the demand arose from the employer's right to direct the enterprise under Article 2 paragraph 2, as interpreted in Awards 141/08-Bloomtime and 38/09-AIA, under which sewing machines and similar equipment are company assets the employer may lawfully relocate, and it found the transport was a genuine reallocation between factories [Para 114][Para 115][Para 116][Para 121].

## 5. Ratio decidendi
Where an employer has no work at all for piece-rate workers on undetermined duration contracts, it commits no serious offence under Article 83 part A point 5, and the workers cannot compel it to dismiss them; but a prolonged work stoppage with reduced earnings is conduct that pushes the workers to resign, so on resignation they are entitled to the Article 89 indemnity as damages under Article 90, plus outstanding wages and unused annual leave [Para 82][Para 92][Para 101].
An employer may relocate machinery and materials between its establishments as an incident of its right to direct the enterprise; the workers' loss of trust does not make the transport unlawful [Para 116][Para 121].

**Obiter dicta:** The panel recorded that the employer attributed its loss of orders to frequent strikes and the resulting air freight costs, but that finding played no part in the orders made [Para 77].

## 6. Source defects disclosed
Wage figures are printed inconsistently: a US$ 61 main wage with a US$ 5 attendance bonus and seniority bonus, described later as "full wages, attendance bonuses, and seniority bonuses, amounting to US$ 71 per month" [Para 27][Para 96]. Machine counts appear as "approximately 1,300", "only 1,229 sewing machines" and "more than 300 sewing machines" needed elsewhere [Para 22][Para 56][Para 58]. The workforce is given as "approximately 550 workers", then "545 workers" holding undetermined duration contracts, and both 545 and 550 authorisations are mentioned [Para 20][Para 59]. Paragraph 80 is corrupted mid-sentence ("the workers cannot force A…"), losing the start of the Article 83 analysis. The second-hearing notices are dated 7 January 2011, three weeks before the first hearing of 28 January 2011 [Para 18]. The 6 January 2011 conciliation minutes are listed both as employer evidence and as Ministry evidence [Para 16][Para 17].
