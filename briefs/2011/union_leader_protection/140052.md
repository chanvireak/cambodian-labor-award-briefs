# Award No. 91/11 — Kwik Shoe (Cambodia) Co., Ltd. (Arbitration Council, 8 August 2011)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators (Seng Vouch Hun, An Non, Ang Eng Thong) · Doc ID 140052

## 1. Facts
The Movement Trade Union (សម្ព័ន្ធ សហជីព ចលនា កម្មករ) filed a complaint with the Labour Dispute Department on 10 June 2011, alleging that Kwik Shoe (Cambodia) Co., Ltd.—a garment factory employing approximately 1,320 workers—had engaged in anti-union discrimination. [Para 11][Para 42][Para 43] The dispute arose from the company's decision to terminate Ms K on 09 June 2011, several weeks before her fixed-term contract was due to expire on 08 July 2011. [Para 45][Para 47][Para 48] Ms K had been employed at the factory since 08 October 2008 as a sewer, initially on three-month contracts that were later extended to six-month terms. [Para 45][Para 46] At the time of her dismissal, she held two distinct roles: she was a deputy worker representative (ប្រតិភូ ជំនួយ), elected by the workforce, and she had been appointed president of the factory's Movement Trade Union on 14 May 2011, a change the union formally notified to the employer. [Para 51][Para 56][Para 57] The company terminated her employment after security guards summoned her to the administration office, where officials informed her that there was no more work and that her contract was being cut short; they confiscated her identification card on the spot. [Para 52][Para 53][Para 54] The employer neither provided prior notice of the termination nor notified the Labour Inspector, as required for protected workers. [Para 55] While the parties resolved a related dispute over the deduction of union dues during conciliation, the reinstatement claim remained unresolved and was referred to the panel. [Para 16][Para 17]

## 2. Issues in dispute
The panel framed the sole remaining issue as follows:
1. Whether the employer's termination of Ms K, a deputy worker representative, without prior authorisation from the Labour Inspector violated Article 293 of the Labour Law, entitling her to reinstatement and back pay? [Para 64][Para 75]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** The union contended that Ms K was a protected deputy worker representative and the newly elected president of the factory's union, and that the company dismissed her abruptly on 09 June 2011 without notice, without cause, and without seeking the Labour Inspector's approval, demanding her reinstatement and back pay. [Para 2][Para 52][Para 55][Para 56]
**Employer (respondent):** The company asserted that it terminated Ms K for legitimate performance-related reasons, including selling clothes and operating a public telephone for workers during working hours; it claimed it had issued a verbal warning in April 2011, that it was unaware of her election as union president, and that it had paid her wages in accordance with the Labour Law. [Para 59][Para 61][Para 62][Para 63]

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** Granted — the panel ordered the employer to reinstate Ms K at her original workplace and to pay her back wages from the date of her dismissal. [Para 81]
The panel began by quoting Article 293 of the Labour Law (1997), which states that the dismissal of a worker delegate or any other elected representative may only be carried out with the agreement of the Labour Inspector, and that the Inspector must issue a decision within one month of receiving the request. [Para 64][Para 65][Para 66] The panel emphasised that this provision grants protected status to worker delegates and requires employers to obtain administrative approval before any termination. [Para 67][Para 68] To determine whether Ms K qualified as a protected delegate, the panel turned to Prakas No. 286 (2001), which mandates that enterprises with eight or more workers establish a system of worker representatives. [Para 70] The Prakas specifies that each full delegate (ប្រតិភូ ពេញ សិទ្ធិ) must have a deputy delegate (ប្រតិភូ ជំនួយ), and that both are elected by the workers for two-year terms. [Para 71][Para 72] The panel reasoned that because deputy delegates are elected representatives with a statutory mandate, they fall within the class of "worker delegates" protected by Article 293. [Para 73][Para 74] Applying this framework, the panel found that Ms K was a deputy worker representative and that the employer had terminated her without notifying the Labour Inspector or obtaining the required authorisation. [Para 75] It rejected the employer's argument that it was unaware of her union presidency, noting that her status as a deputy representative was sufficient to trigger protection, and concluded that the dismissal was procedurally invalid. [Para 76][Para 77] The panel cited its prior awards No. 71/09 and No. 09/10, which held that an employer who fails to seek the Labour Inspector's approval must reinstate the dismissed worker. [Para 78][Para 79] Accordingly, the panel ordered the employer to reinstate Ms K at her original workplace and to pay her back wages from the date of dismissal. [Para 80][Para 81]

## 5. Ratio decidendi
A deputy worker representative (ប្រតិភូ ជំនួយ), as defined by Prakas No. 286, is a protected worker delegate under Article 293 of the Labour Law; an employer must obtain prior authorisation from the Labour Inspector before terminating such a representative, and a dismissal carried out without that authorisation is invalid, entitling the worker to reinstatement at the original workplace with back pay from the date of dismissal. [Para 73][Para 77][Para 81]

**Obiter dicta:** The panel noted the employer's claim that it was unaware of Ms K's appointment as union president, but did not resolve this factual dispute, as her independent status as a deputy worker representative was sufficient to trigger the protection of Article 293. [Para 63][Para 69]
