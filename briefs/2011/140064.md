# Award No. 104/11 — Winner Nei Thing Factory (Arbitration Council, 15 September 2011)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators (Oeng Sothy for the employer party, An Vireak for the worker party, Kong Phal R as chair) · Doc ID 140064

## 1. Facts
Winner Nei Thing Factory, a garment factory in Kandal province, employed 1,920 workers; the factory branch of the Cambodia Democratic Garment Workers' Union Federation, the claimant, had 836 members and a second-term leadership recognised by the Ministry on 22 July 2011. [Para 52][Para 53][Para 54] Two workers, both hired in 1998 in the ironing section on indefinite-duration contracts, were dismissed by the company on 12 March 2011; both were union activists and neither had previously been warned. [Para 57][Para 58][Para 59][Para 60][Para 65][Para 66] Conciliation failed on six of nine demands on 21 June 2011; four were settled at the hearing of 23 August 2011, leaving the reinstatement demand and the piece-rate notice demand. [Para 18][Para 21][Para 16]

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. (panel's issue 3) Whether the employer must take back the two dismissed union activists, or instead pay them termination compensation? [Para 55][Para 85]
2. (panel's issue 4) Whether the employer must post the piece rate (តម្លៃឡូត៍) for each new series before the workers perform the work, rather than on the 25th of the month? [Para 74][Para 127]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** the two were dismissed for their union activity — one was an activist who dealt with the administration and argued with the section chief — with no valid reason and no prior warning; the demand for advance notice of piece rates rests on the uncertainty workers face when a new series starts and rates are posted only at month end. [Para 56][Para 62][Para 64][Para 75][Para 76]
**Employer (respondent):** the dismissal concerned only two individuals and was a unilateral act intended to prevent a bad example, not a collective dispute; both workers took excessive leave and did not cooperate when work was pressing; the company chose not to invoke any fault and volunteered notice pay, annual-leave pay, dismissal indemnity and moral damages, which the two refused; rates cannot be posted within three days because many series are priced at once and pricing at month end avoids repeated disputes. [Para 55][Para 71][Para 72][Para 73][Para 77]

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** partly granted — reinstatement was refused and the employer was ordered to pay the workers their termination compensation; and the employer was ordered to post each new series' piece rate before the work is done, within 3 to 7 days after the workers finish a new series. [Para 141][Para 142]

The panel first satisfied itself that it had jurisdiction. [Para 87][Para 88] It held the dispute collective under Article 302: it arose between two workers and their employer over a working condition; it was brought by a federation representing 836 workers; and the employer produced no evidence against the third condition, so it was presumed. [Para 93][Para 95][Para 96][Para 97][Para 98][Para 99][Para 100][Para 101] It also recorded that the parties had, by the memorandum of understanding of 28 September 2010, agreed that rights-dispute decisions would bind them, while interest-dispute decisions would not. [Para 22][Para 23][Para 27]

On reinstatement, Article 74 permits an employer to terminate an indefinite-duration contract but only for a reason relating to the worker's aptitude or conduct, or to the operational needs of the enterprise. [Para 103][Para 105][Para 106] The panel found the only reason given was that the two had taken a lot of leave, that the employer disclaimed any fault, and that the termination was therefore an act of the employer's will unsupported by such a reason. [Para 108][Para 109][Para 110] Article 91 then entitled the workers to moral damages, which the panel noted are distinct from notice pay and dismissal indemnity. [Para 111][Para 112] It found the employer had in fact paid notice pay, annual-leave pay, dismissal indemnity and moral damages, and that the workers had simply refused to accept them. [Para 113][Para 114][Para 115] On the union-discrimination allegation, the burden lay on the workers, and their only evidence was the general assertion about one worker's dealings with the administration; that was insufficient, so the dismissal was not shown to be anti-union. [Para 118][Para 123][Para 124][Para 125] Reinstatement was refused and payment of the termination compensation ordered. [Para 126]

On the piece rate, Article 112(a) requires the employer to inform workers clearly of the wage conditions before they take up a post and at every change of those conditions; the article fixes no number of days, but the obligation is unambiguous. [Para 127][Para 128][Para 129][Para 130] Following award 62/04 E-Sun, the panel held the number of days must be judged by the employer's practical requirements, and noted that earlier awards had settled on a range of three to seven days for setting lot prices. [Para 131][Para 132][Para 133] It rejected the employer's account that it priced everything at month end, finding the real motive was fear of wage protests, and that silence left workers unable to know how their pay would change. [Para 136][Para 137][Para 138][Para 139] The employer was therefore ordered to make the rate known before the work on a lot, within 3 to 7 days after a new series is completed. [Para 140][Para 142]

## 5. Ratio decidendi
An indefinite-duration contract may be terminated under Article 74 only for a reason of aptitude, conduct or operational necessity; where the employer disclaims fault and relies on repeated leave-taking, the termination is an exercise of will without such a reason, and Article 91 moral damages follow — but the panel will not order reinstatement, and compensation already tendered discharges the obligation. A claim of anti-union dismissal fails unless the worker produces specific facts, the burden lying on the worker. [Para 106][Para 109][Para 111][Para 118][Para 123]
Under Article 112(a) the employer must post the piece rate for each new series before the work begins, within three to seven days of the workers completing a new series; the article sets no deadline itself, but month-end pricing that leaves workers ignorant of their pay until after the work is done breaches the duty to inform. [Para 129][Para 130][Para 133][Para 140]

**Obiter dicta:** the observations that the employer's stated reason for month-end pricing was in truth anxiety about wage protests [Para 137], and that moral damages are conceptually separate from notice pay and dismissal indemnity [Para 112], are explanatory and not part of the order.

## 6. Source defects disclosed
- The reasoning is internally contradictory on the lawfulness of the dismissal: at [Para 109]–[Para 110] the panel finds the termination unsupported by any valid reason and "not correct", while at [Para 115] it concludes the employer "fulfilled its obligations under the Labour Law". Both are reported; the operative order follows the second. [Para 141]
- The jurisdictional findings contain a numerical slip: the panel says the dispute is between two workers and "4 employers" [Para 96], and elsewhere describes 836 members as the affected group [Para 98].
- The chair's name is legible only as "Kong Phal R" in the header [Para 1] and is unrecoverable in the signature block, which is overwritten by scanner noise [Para 145]; the employer-party arbitrator's printed name differs between header and signature block. The panel composition is therefore reported only in part.
- The operative order names a third, otherwise unknown worker, rendering the claimant's name as "Lind" [Para 141], an artefact of the scan.
- Procedural figures do not reconcile: six of nine demands are said to have failed conciliation [Para 18], yet six points are recorded as unresolved and then four settled with two remaining [Para 19][Para 21].
- Both dismissed workers, the employer's administration chief and the union officials are named in the source; all appear here by role or initial only.
