# Award No. 162/15 & 214/15 — Smile of Angkor (Siem Reap (Cambodia) Performance Means and Art Investment Co., Ltd) (Arbitration Council, 20 October 2015)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · panel of 3: Chiv Phirom (employer-appointed), An Vira (worker-appointed), Pen Bun Chhea (chair, appointed by the two members) · Doc ID 139125

## 1. Facts
The company, registered in 2010, stages Khmer artistic performances at Siem Reap and employed 63 staff. [Para 184]
Artists were recruited and evaluated jointly by company trainers, a Chinese instructor and two intermediaries, Ms S (a Ministry of Culture official) and Mr C, who were themselves paid monthly by the company; the company paid one lump sum which Ms S divided among the artists. [Para 196]
A union was elected on 28 March 2015 and the company then required artists to sign new 2015 contracts, withdraw their complaints and leave the union. Fifty-four artists and staff transferred their claims to the Tourism and Services Workers' Federation of Cambodia; 26 of 27 demands failed conciliation. [Para 54]
Both sides elected a non-binding award. [Para 73]

## 2. Issues in dispute
In case 162/15 the panel considered issues 02 & 27 (consolidated), 04, 06, 07, 08, 19 and 26; in case 214/15 it considered issues 01, 04, 05 and 06 (consolidated). [Para 61][Para 70]
1. Whether the company is the employer of the artists, or only a client of an intermediary contractor under Article 45? [Para 403]
2. Whether the six dismissed union leaders and activists were dismissed because of their union activity and must be reinstated with back pay? [Para 502]
3. Whether the remaining 21 artists and two staff were dismissed and must also be reinstated? [Para 481]
4. Whether the company must adopt internal rules under Articles 22–24? [Para 512]
5. Whether the company owed weekly rest-day pay, public-holiday pay and annual leave for the last three years? [Para 526]
6. Whether the company must provide a first-aid post and register all staff with the National Social Security Fund? [Para 586]
7. Whether a 13th-month salary is owed? [Para 621]
8. Whether the company discriminated against four pregnant artists? [Para 640]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** The company was the real employer: it selected, paid, disciplined and dismissed them. Dismissals began immediately after the union was formed and targeted only its leaders; the company told them to withdraw the complaint and quit the union to keep their jobs. They sought reinstatement with back pay, internal rules, three years of rest-day, holiday and annual-leave arrears, a first-aid post, NSSF registration and a 13th month. [Para 268]
**Employer (respondent):** It was not the employer, having only annual rental contracts with Ms S and Mr C, who recruited and managed the artists; it paid one service fee. It denied anti-union motive, saying those not re-hired had failed to sign the new contract, and denied pregnancy discrimination, noting it bought insurance for all artists. [Para 214]

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** partly granted. The company was declared the employer; it was ordered to reinstate six dismissed artists with back pay and benefits from dismissal to reinstatement, and the two staff plus 21 artists in case 214/15 on the same terms; to adopt internal rules; to pay three years of arrears for weekly rest days, public holidays and annual leave to 32 artists and staff; to establish a first-aid post under Prakas No. 330; and to register all staff with the NSSF. The 13th-month claim was refused consideration and the pregnancy-discrimination claim dismissed. [Para 677][Para 686]
On employment status, the panel applied the three Article 45 conditions — genuine autonomy in recruiting, a global service price, and a written contract — and found the first two unmet: recruitment required the company's participation and consent, and the lump sum was not a global price but the pre-set individual wages bundled for distribution. Ms S and Mr C were therefore staff, not contractors, and the company was the employer under Articles 2 and 3. [Para 425][Para 435]
On the dismissals, the panel found the company, not the intermediaries, made the decisions, and that the reason given by the company's own witnesses was participation in union activity; three artists were re-hired only after renouncing the union, which confirmed the motive. Article 12 and Article 279 prohibit using union membership or activity as a ground for dismissal, so reinstatement with back pay was ordered under Prakas 099 Article 34. [Para 502][Para 511]
Internal rules were ordered because the company employed 63, above the eight-employee threshold, and admitted having none. On rest days the panel found staff worked with only one day off a month, held the company's practice unlawful, and awarded arrears for the statutory three-year limitation period, at 100% of normal pay for work on the weekly rest day and 200% for public holidays. Annual leave was awarded under Article 166 at 18 days a year, one extra day per three years' seniority. [Para 521]
The first-aid post was ordered under Article 238 and Prakas 330; insurance cover did not discharge that duty. NSSF registration was ordered because the company had over eight employees and had paid no occupational-risk contributions. [Para 619]
The 13th month was held to be an interest dispute, which the panel could not hear because the claimant was not the most representative union; the pregnancy claim failed for want of evidence, the four dismissals being attributed to union activity or non-signature of the new contract. [Para 637][Para 663]

## 5. Ratio decidendi
Where a company fixes each worker's wage itself and pays a lump sum to an intermediary only for distribution, and where recruitment requires the company's own participation and consent, the intermediary is not an independent contractor under Article 45 but a staff member, and the company is the employer — so dismissals carried out on its instruction engage Articles 12 and 279 and require reinstatement with back pay. [Para 425][Para 435][Para 502]

**Obiter dicta:** The panel noted that buying accident insurance for artists, though a mark of the employer's care, does not discharge the separate statutory duty to maintain a first-aid post in the workplace. It also observed that only a union with majority membership may bring an interest dispute for hearing. [Para 603]

## 6. Source defects disclosed
OCR damage is extensive: witness lists at paras 1–5 are duplicated and garbled, with names repeated and numbers lost; contract schedules at paras 77–151 repeat several entries and misdate some to "2019". The employee count is printed as 63 at paras 185 and 521, as 630 at para 600 and as 54 claimants at para 191. The number of dismissed artists appears as 21, 23 and 27 in paras 670–672 and as "212" at para 686. The union president is recorded both as a candidate and as the winner at paras 263–265, and several individual names are lost to a bare initial or digit.
