# Award No. 031/18 — Ken Sport Shoe Co., Ltd. (Arbitration Council, 13 September 2018)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators (Ing Sothy, appointed by the employer; Liv Sovanna, appointed by the workers; Pen Bunhea, president, appointed by both) · Doc ID 138913

## 1. Facts
Ken Sport Shoe Co., Ltd. runs a shoe factory at Chamkar Svay village, Sethei commune, Samaki Mean Chey district, Kampong Chhnang province, employing approximately 7,303 workers [Para 45]. A collective labour dispute was referred after conciliation at the Provincial Department of Labour and Vocational Training left 4 of 9 worker demands unresolved [Para 13]. At the hearing the parties settled issues 1–3, leaving only the union-dues demand for decision [Para 18]. The Cambodia Union at Ken Sport Shoe held a registration certificate from the Provincial Department [Para 33]; both parties accepted that no most-representative union yet existed in the enterprise [Para 49].

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. Whether the company must grant workers legal permission to be absent when they have personal errands? [Para 2]
2. Whether the company must stop requiring workers to take annual leave during periods without work, and pay them in lieu of leave already taken? [Para 3]
3. Whether the company must cease discrimination against the Cambodia Union? [Para 4]
4. Whether the employer is obliged to deduct 1,000 riel per month from the wages of each Cambodia Union member, to be paid to the union, from September 2018 onwards? [Para 86]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** The employer is bound to deduct wages to pay union dues once the worker has consented; no law bars the union from filing the deduction request itself, and it attached the workers' consent letters [Para 59]. Workers dare not approach the administration individually because management questions their union membership and discourages it [Para 60].

**Employer (respondent):** It will deduct only when each member worker personally delivers the written consent to the administration, alone or with a union representative [Para 61]. The earlier practice caused complaints from workers who said they had never consented, so management changed it [Para 102].

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** granted — the employer was ordered to deduct 1,000 riel per member per month from the wages of Cambodia Union members and pay it to the union, whenever the union files a deduction request accompanied by dated consent letters bearing the workers' signatures or thumbprints [Para 108].

The panel first confirmed that the three worker representatives recognised by the Provincial Department of Labour held valid mandates, following its earlier awards on representative status, and proceeded to the merits [Para 84]. On issue 4 it applied Article 129 of the Labour Law, which voids collective agreements permitting wage deductions outside the listed cases but expressly allows a worker to authorise deduction of wages to pay dues to a union of which he is a member, in writing and revocably at any time [Para 87]. It read this with Article 26(1) of the Law on Trade Unions (2016), under which the employer must deduct and transfer dues automatically once written consent exists, in the manner fixed by ministerial prakas [Para 91]. Prakas No. 300 of 2 July 2018, Article 1, permits a properly registered union to request deduction from its members, and the panel found the Cambodia Union so registered [Para 95]. Article 4(2) of the same prakas requires written notice to the employer at least 10 days before deduction begins, attaching dated consents signed or thumbprinted by the member, which the union had done in April 2018 [Para 100][Para 101]. The panel found no law or legal instrument requiring the worker to lodge the request with the employer personally, and held the employer's insistence on personal lodgement contrary to Prakas No. 300 [Para 105].

## 5. Ratio decidendi
Where a duly registered union files a written request at least 10 days in advance, attaching dated consents signed or thumbprinted by each member, the employer must deduct the stated dues from those members' wages and remit them to the union; the employer may not condition deduction on each member appearing in person, since neither the Labour Law, the Law on Trade Unions nor Prakas No. 300 imposes that formality [Para 106].

**Obiter dicta:** The panel's remarks that only a most-representative union holds exclusive rights in collective bargaining and collective dispute resolution under Article 54(1) of the Law on Trade Unions were not necessary to the outcome, since it found no most-representative union existed at the enterprise [Para 69][Para 72].

## 6. Source defects disclosed
Two dates are printed twice with different years: the agreed extension of the award date runs from 7 September "2019 2018" to 13 September 2018 [Para 21], and Prakas No. 300 is dated 2 July "2019 2018" [Para 98]. The conciliation report is dated both 1 and 15 August 2018 in the same sentence [Para 8]. Membership figures conflict: the union asserted 167 consenting members while the attached consents totalled 154 [Para 65]. The workforce is printed as 7,303, which may be an OCR corruption of a smaller figure [Para 45]. The union appears under two identifiers — certificate No. 1373 and registration No. 1041700225, both dated 28 December 2017 [Para 33]. Representative names in the recital at [Para 82] cannot be reconciled with the list elsewhere in the same award. The signature block omits Ing Sothy and attributes Liv Sovanna to the employer side, contrary to the composition on the face of the award [Para 110].
