# Award No. 21/10 — Cambodian Organisation to Help Women in Distress (AKSV) (Arbitration Council, 23 April 2010)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators (Mar Samborana for the employer, Huon Chundy for the workers, Nhan Somonty chair) · Doc ID 139771

## 1. Facts
The respondent is a non-governmental, non-profit organisation founded in 1997, running a project against violence against women (VAW) from offices in Phnom Penh, Siem Reap and Banteay Meanchey. [Para 104] In June 2009 it terminated its staff for lack of project budget; the panel found 30 dismissals against the employer's figure of 29, and seven long-serving employees on successive fixed-duration contracts remained as claimants after one settled at the Ministry. [Para 106][Para 108][Para 110] They claimed wages said to remain on their contracts, the severance and 13th-month benefits printed at page 27 of the internal regulations, statutory benefits and USD 5,000 moral damages each, and at the hearing dropped any claim to reinstatement. [Para 109][Para 158]

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. Whether the seven claimants' successive fixed-duration contracts had become indefinite-duration contracts? [Para 159][Para 176]
2. Whether the June 2009 dismissals were a collective redundancy under Article 95, and whether Ms D's resignation was a constructive dismissal under Article 90? [Para 189][Para 188]
3. Whether the claimants are entitled to wages said to remain on their fixed-duration contracts and to the severance and 13th-month benefits in the internal regulations? [Para 206][Para 208]
4. Whether the claimants are entitled to USD 5,000 moral damages each under Article 91, or to compensation for the employer's failure to observe the Article 95 procedure? [Para 242][Para 273]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** the budget excuse is untrue — the former director said the Phnom Penh office was only slightly short in 2009, reserves existed from souvenir sales, health-fund deductions and bonuses, and project budgets leave balances at year end; new staff were recruited for the same projects after the dismissals, and Ms D was forced to write a resignation. [Para 115][Para 116][Para 122][Para 136]
**Employer (respondent):** it could not pay the balance of the contracts or USD 5,000 because it dismissed staff before donor cash arrived, and some donors who had signed never paid as promised; the six others had served over two years, so their contracts had become indefinite and termination followed those terms; Ms D resigned voluntarily and was paid. [Para 111][Para 113][Para 118][Para 120][Para 134]

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** partly granted — the claim to wages remaining on the contracts was dismissed, while the employer was ordered to pay the internal-regulation benefits (5% of total income and 13th-month pay, pro rata for the part-timer), severance calculated on those regulations under Articles 13 and 89, pay in lieu of notice to all seven, two months' wages to six workers for the Article 95 breach, Article 91 damages to Ms D alone, unused annual leave for the last three years and unpaid final wages; the USD 5,000 each was refused. [Para 317][Para 328]

Article 67(2) caps a fixed-duration contract at two years and permits renewal only while the renewal itself stays within two years, any breach converting the contract to indefinite duration; Article 73(5) requires 10 days' notice of expiry for contracts over six months and 15 days over one year, failing which the contract runs on for an equal term or becomes indefinite once the total passes two years. [Para 159][Para 162] Following its awards 10/03, 36/06 and 57/06, the panel read the cap as applying to the aggregate of successive contracts rather than to each renewal, and found that all seven held three successive contracts exceeding two years in total. [Para 165][Para 177]

Ms D signed a resignation on 2 June 2009 after being told that dismissal for leaking confidential documents would bring no compensation; the employer admitted the inducement but produced no evidence of any leak, so the panel held the resignation did not express her will and treated it as a constructive dismissal under Article 90. [Para 184][Para 188] The other five were dismissed unilaterally on 30 June 2009 together with 25 colleagues for the same budget reason, which the panel classified as a collective redundancy under Article 95. [Para 193][Para 198]

Because the contracts were indefinite, no term remained to be paid and the contractual-balance claim failed. [Para 207] Page 27 of the internal regulations nonetheless gave 15 days' wages per year of service capped at six months, plus 5% of total income and 13th-month pay; all seven had over two years' service and the part-timer took a pro-rata share. [Para 213] The panel used the regulation's last-month wage base instead of the Article 89 twelve-month average because it was more favourable, which Article 13 allows. [Para 238][Para 241]

Article 91 damages presuppose termination without valid reason, so the six dismissed for a genuine shortfall failed, while Ms D, constructively dismissed without reason, succeeded. [Para 251][Para 252] The employer had not notified the labour inspector or the staff representative as Article 95 requires; that omission does not itself ground Article 91 damages, but it deprived the six of the chance of a ministerial suspension of up to 30 days twice, for which the panel awarded two months' wages. [Para 266][Para 273] Paid job-search days under Article 79 were refused because none of the seven had asked for them during notice. [Para 301]

## 5. Ratio decidendi
Successive fixed-duration contracts whose aggregate duration exceeds two years become a single indefinite-duration contract, and the employer then owes the severance, 13th-month and percentage benefits its own internal regulations attach to termination without fault, calculated on the last month's wage where that is more favourable than the Article 89 average. A resignation the employer procures to avoid paying severance is a constructive dismissal under Article 90, and a dismissal for a genuine budget shortfall is a collective redundancy under Article 95 that bars Article 91 damages — but failure to notify the labour inspector of that redundancy entitles each affected worker to two months' wages. [Para 177][Para 188][Para 241][Para 252][Para 273]

**Obiter dicta:** the panel's reading of ILO Recommendation No. 166 of 1982, paragraph 30, as useful but non-binding context for Article 67 [Para 170][Para 172]; and its remark that an award on an interest dispute would replace the collective agreement and remove the right to strike [Para 95].
