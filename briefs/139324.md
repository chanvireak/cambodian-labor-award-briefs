# Award No. 52/04 — Cinkamp Apparel Corp (Arbitration Council, 22 July 2004)
Forum: Arbitration Council of Cambodia · 3 arbitrators — Mar Samborana (employer-chosen), Tuon Siphann (worker-chosen), Kong Phallack (chair) · Doc ID 139324

## 1. Facts
Cinkamp Apparel Corp employed 720 workers at a factory newly relocated to Prey Pring Village, Chom Chao quarter, Dangkor district, Phnom Penh. On 22 May 2004 the Cambodian Workers Federation complained to the Dangkor labour inspection office on ten points; conciliation settled seven, and the remainder went to the Arbitration Council on 1 July 2004. [Para 20][Para 21][Para 22] The dispute that survived to hearing concerned three demands: that security guards stop carrying guns inside the factory, that seniority wage increases be paid, and that Administration Manager Mr I Sineng be dismissed. [Para 12][Para 13]

## 2. Issues in dispute
1. Whether the company's security guards should be prohibited from using guns in the factory? [Para 12]
2. Whether the company should increase the wages of permanent workers by seniority — USD5 a month at six months to one year, USD7 at one to two years and USD10 beyond two years? [Para 13]
3. Whether the company should dismiss its Administration Manager, Mr I Sineng, over his conduct toward the union vice-president? [Para 13]

## 3. Positions of the parties
**Workers (claimant):** Guards carrying guns frightened the workers; other factories armed guards only with sticks, and the union treated the weaponry as intimidation. They argued the company was profitable because headcount had grown from 400–500 to about 700, so seniority increases were affordable. On the manager, they said Mr S twice threatened Ms V, that on 15 June 2004 he told her to stop working or he would ask "Brother Heng" to act, and that she was then moved from number punching to pocket stamping and suspended a week — a change the union read as retaliation for her union office, since the other five workers in her team were not union members. [Para 23][Para 26][Para 29][Para 45][Para 49]

**Employer (respondent):** The guns protected workers and the company from armed gangsters around the new site and had never been used to threaten anyone. The company had already applied the MoSALVY announcement on seniority increments of USD2 to USD5, and its financial report showed no profit, so the demanded scale was unaffordable; relocation and heat-absorbing fans had consumed its capital. It refused to dismiss Mr S, saying he had done nothing wrong and had sworn as much, though it conceded he asked Ms V to change jobs, suspended her seven days for refusing, told Mr H not to readmit her pending resolution, and used the bouncing-ball analogy. [Para 54][Para 56][Para 59][Para 60][Para 66][Para 70]

## 4. Award and reasoning
**Outcome:** partly granted — the panel ordered both parties to perform the settlement they reached at the hearing (guards may carry guns at the gates but not visibly in the campus nor at all in the factory or work areas; a US$10 attendance-linked bonus each half-year from 1 July 2004, first payable in June and second in December, plus USD5 at the end of December 2004 for workers holding the monthly bonus January–December 2004), and rejected the demand to dismiss Mr I Sineng. [Para 136][Para 137][Para 138][Para 139]

On the guns and the bonus the parties agreed in the hearing, and the panel simply recorded and ordered compliance with that agreement rather than deciding the merits; the earlier conciliation had left these points open, and the panel's award converted the in-hearing settlement into an enforceable order. [Para 15][Para 76][Para 136]

On the transfer and suspension, the panel found the facts as the workers described them: Mr S told Ms V to quit the union or resign, she refused, she was moved from punching numbers to stamping pockets, she refused the transfer, and she was suspended one week. The panel held this was discrimination on the ground of union membership. Article 279 of the Labour Law forbids an employer from taking union affiliation or participation into account in recruitment, management and assignment of work, promotion, remuneration, benefits, disciplinary measures and dismissal; punishing Ms V by changing her work and suspending her for union involvement rather than misconduct therefore breached that article, and the employer "must be held responsible before the law". Because the workers claimed no damages and asked only for the manager's dismissal, the panel made no damages award. [Para 112][Para 117][Para 119][Para 120][Para 121]

On the dismissal demand, the panel held it inconsistent with lawful termination procedure and, more fundamentally, outside its power: it has no jurisdiction to order an employer to dismiss employees, including managers. Article 65 makes the labour contract subject to ordinary law, and Article 22 of Decree-Law No. 38 treats the contract as law between the parties, alterable only by consent, so hiring and dismissing is the employer's right; even where Article 83 serious misconduct exists, the Labour Law gives the employer a right to dismiss, not a duty. The panel noted a narrow exception — it may order dismissal or transfer of a manager who endangers the health and security of other employees or the company — and found no such danger here, following a line of prior awards. [Para 125][Para 126][Para 128][Para 131][Para 133][Para 135]

## 5. Ratio decidendi
Where an employer transfers a union officer to different work and then suspends her after she refuses the transfer, and the sequence follows her refusal to leave her union post, the measure is discrimination on the ground of union membership contrary to Article 279 of the Labour Law, whatever disciplinary colour it carries. Separately, the panel will not order an employer to dismiss a manager at a union's request: dismissal is the employer's contractual right, and the panel's jurisdiction reaches only managers who endanger the health or security of employees or the company. [Para 117][Para 120][Para 126]

**Obiter dicta:** The panel observed that the workers could have claimed damages for the Article 279 breach but chose not to, and so it expressed no view on quantum; it also remarked that internal work rules and the Labour Law bind union members "just like other workers", which was not necessary to the outcome. [Para 71][Para 76][Para 121]

## 6. Source defects disclosed
The OCR is heavily degraded throughout, dropping initial letters and spaces ("themployees", "athemployees", "withemployees"), so quoted statutory language was reconstructed only where the passage was legible. The award date is printed as 22 July 2004 in the header, yet paragraph 23 states the Council summoned the parties to a first hearing on 1 July 2004 at 2 pm, while paragraph 20 records hearings on 7 and 12 July 2004; both readings are reported and neither is corrected here. Paragraph 15 says conciliation succeeded on one of three issues with two unsuccessful, but paragraph 76 records the parties agreeing points 1 and 2 at the hearing, leaving all three resolved. The employee party's registration certificate is dated 26 September 2004, after the award itself, and is reproduced as printed. The decision's numbering runs 1, 1.2, 1.3, so sub-point 1.1 is lost. Individual workers and representatives appear under bare initials or garbled names; only the arbitrators and the Administration Manager's name survive intact. [Para 15][Para 23][Para 76][Para 136]
