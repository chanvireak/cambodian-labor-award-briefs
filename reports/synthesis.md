# What the awards actually disagree about

*AI-generated research notes, not legal advice. Every claim below is a reading of the briefs in this
repository, and each named case links to the brief it comes from.*

Two thousand awards from a single arbitration institution, over twenty years, ought to produce a settled
body of doctrine. They do not. The corpus shows three fault lines, and each one recurs often enough to be
a structural feature of Cambodian labour arbitration rather than an accident of individual panels.

## 1. The most-representative union gate swallows the merits

The single most consequential rule in this corpus is not in the Labour Law's remedies chapter; it is the
standing rule. A union that is not "most representative" in the enterprise cannot bring an **interest**
dispute at all — a demand for more than the law, a collective agreement, internal rules or established
practice already require. The panel does not weigh the demand; it declines to consider it.

What makes this a fault line rather than a rule is how much of an award's substance can be reclassified as
"interest". A demand for a meal allowance above the notification rate, a wage rise beyond the statutory
floor, an extra attendance bonus, a travel allowance — each is refused on standing, not on merit. The same
demand, brought by a union holding the certificate, is decided on the evidence. Two awards with identical
facts can therefore reach opposite results without either panel contradicting the other. See 139406 and
140640 for the rule stated plainly, and 140419 for a refusal that turns entirely on it.

## 2. Procedure decides more cases than substance

A striking number of dismissals in this corpus are unlawful for a reason that has nothing to do with
whether the worker did anything wrong: the Labour Inspector was not asked first. Article 71(11) suspensions
require prior written approval, and Article 293 requires the same before dismissing a protected union
leader. Panels treat approval obtained afterwards as no approval at all.

The consequence is that the merits are never reached. An employer that can prove misconduct loses because
it acted on Friday and wrote to the Inspector on Monday. Conversely, a worker whose conduct was plainly
poor can win reinstatement because the file is missing a letter. 140047 and 140491 state the suspension
rule; 140640's neighbours in the corpus show the same pattern for dismissals. This is the clearest example
in the corpus of procedure operating as a substantive rule rather than a gateway to one.

## 3. Fixed-duration contracts drift into indefinite ones

The two-year rule — renewals whose total passes two years convert a fixed-duration contract into an
undetermined-duration one — is applied consistently and catches employers who use rolling short contracts
to avoid severance. 140489 is the clearest statement.

The disagreement is at the edges: whether the two years aggregate across successive employers on the same
site, whether a contract that ends by agreement before the inspector counts as a renewal, and what notice a
non-renewal requires when the employer's own internal rules demand one. Where the panel finds the
conversion, the remedial consequence is largely mechanical — severance, notice pay, and Article 91 damages
follow. Where it does not, the same facts produce a dismissal.

## What this means for a reader

Cite these briefs for the shape of an argument, not for a holding. These are first-instance arbitration
awards: panels cite each other as persuasive practice rather than binding authority. A ratio recorded here is a panel's reasoning on one set of facts, reproduced
faithfully and pinned to a paragraph, which is exactly what a summary can honestly offer.
